THE SHIFT IS OVER

    What the paper says

    Every formula here gives each path a number, and the dot takes the best path. Each time the formula does not care about a change with a knob on it (how far right, how much turned, how much later), you win a prize: a number that stays the same along the best path and on no other. That is Noether's first theorem, and it runs backwards too: a real prize always points to a change the formula does not care about.

    Then the sneakiest change: let the clock run fast, then slow, then fast again, any way you like, a change with a free hand in it instead of a knob. Some formulas do not care even about that. But then there is no prize. What comes out is a rule: the formula's own equations of motion are not independent, and as many of them as there are free hands follow from the rest. That is her second theorem, and it also runs backwards.

    Put the two together. If the free hand contains "start the clock later", then the clock's prize is inside the rule: its number comes out the same on every path, the bad ones too, so it tells you nothing. The paper calls such a prize improper. That is the answer to Hilbert's question. In Einstein's formula for gravity the coordinates can be relabelled freely, moment by moment; "start the clock later" sits inside that freedom; so the energy it gives is empty. Energy is not lost. That formula has no clock of its own to count it with.

    The paper's last word: Hilbert said this failure is characteristic of general relativity. It is, exactly when "general relativity" means a formula that does not care about changes with free hands in them; and it holds for every such formula, in any number of dimensions.

    Both ways

    A real prize means a change with a knob the formula does not care about. An empty prize means a free hand that contains the change you pulled. Either way, the formula and its prizes are the same thing seen from two sides.

    How to play

    Find what the formula doesn't care about.
    Each one wins a number that never changes.

    The formula. Each of the four formulas takes a path and gives it a number. The plate shows the formula and says it in words; x′ means how fast x changes. Of all the paths from A to B, one has the smallest number: the best path. The dot in the window runs along it. The dots on its trail are the ticks of a clock, one per tick.

    Drawing a path. In the first formula you draw a path from A to B with your finger and see its number next to the best path's. Later, after each prize, you draw any path you like to test the prize.

    The changes. Six levers each change the whole drawing in one way: move it right or up, turn it, start the clock later; or, the two magenta levers, re-tick the clock or wobble the drawing by an amount you draw. Tap a lever and the window shows the changed drawing in blue over the old one in grey, and the plate shows the formula's numbers before and after, for the best path and for one other.

    The question. Does the formula care? If the numbers stay the same for every path, it does not, and you win a prize. If you think it does not care, PULL. If the formula does care, the press jams, a blank is spent, and no prize comes.

    The prize. A number you can work out from the dot at any moment, where it is and how fast it goes. The press stamps it on a steel tag with its formula. The needle reads that number as a dot runs along a path.

    The Auditor. A real prize keeps its number along the best path and changes it along paths the dot would never take. An empty prize keeps its number on every path, so it tells you nothing. You test each prize by drawing paths and watching the needle; then the Auditor runs his own paths and stamps PRIZE or EMPTY.

    The free hand. When the formula does not care about a change you drew, no prize comes out. The tag reads RULE: the formula's two equations of motion are not independent. Watch what that does to the clock's prize in that formula.

    The Auditor moves first. In two formulas he already holds a prize and asks which change it comes from, or holds an empty prize and asks which change explains it. Pull the change you think it is; a wrong pull still spends a blank.

    The end. After four formulas the verdict is in words: what you won, what came out empty, and what the paper says it means.
