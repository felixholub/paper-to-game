## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: an argument.** A conflict model of regime complexity (Figures 1 and 2). Where international institutions overlap, their rules can collide; when an actor turns such a collision into a manifest *interface conflict* ("incompatible positional differences between actors about the prevalence of different international norms or rules"), the conflict is managed somehow, and the management has an effect on the order: integration or fragmentation. The hinge is the conflict's origin. A *non-instrumental* interface conflict (NIIC) emerges as a side-effect when a new event or issue meets two institutions with different functional logics (the World Bank's no-politics charter against the UN human-rights office); its parties also value the public goods the institutions provide, so it is "likely to be handled in a cooperative way". An *instrumental* interface conflict (IIC) is created on purpose by an actor who wants to challenge an institution, by pitting another institution's norm against it (Israel and the US against UNRWA, citing UNHCR's refugee definition; the African Union against the UN Security Council over Libya); at least one party "intentionally takes a position incompatible" with the other's, so management is likely non-cooperative, "at least initially". NIICs resemble coordination games; IICs collaboration games, which "can shift toward cooperation over time". Cooperative management tends to integrate, non-cooperative to fragment. Formalised here as a toy bargaining model (section 12): the player is one party; the other party's behaviour is fixed by the clash's origin; the order effect follows the management with the paper's own rates.

**Secondary: an estimate.** Cluster analysis (Gower distance, Ward linkage) of 77 conflicts, 1970–2018, from the ICD 2.0. Table 1: 42 intentional (54.5%) and 35 unintended; 59 managed cooperatively (76.6%); 46 of 76 integrative (60.5%). Simple three-variable solution (Figure 4): cluster 1, "benign", 35 NIICs, 94% cooperative (33), 74% integrative (25); cluster 2, "hybrid", 26 IICs, 100% cooperative, 77% integrative (20); cluster 3, "malign", 16 IICs, 0% cooperative, 6% integrative (1). Note 10: a sub-cluster of 8 NIICs that were cooperative and still fragmenting, and of 6 such IICs. The extended solution (Figure 6) adds object (interests vs values/means), spheres (within vs across spheres of authority) and outcome (symmetric vs asymmetric gains): the three clusters survive; object and outcome do not separate them; spheres does, partly (benign 0% across, hybrid 96%, malign 50%), which the paper leaves as an open puzzle. Reconstructed: the deck of nine clashes reproduces the three clusters in proportion, and the order effect is drawn with the clusters' rates.

**Also handed over: three cases.** Malign: UNRWA against UNHCR norms (2017 on: funding cut, mandate renewed by the General Assembly anyway, no agreement). Hybrid: IRENA (2009) against the IEA: initial mistrust, then the IEA upgraded its renewables unit, a partnership (2012), a memorandum (2019); the paper's explanation is that IRENA's cause "resonated increasingly well with trans- and international audiences", so the IEA faced "a legitimacy crisis" if it kept resisting, and the US joined IRENA in 2011. Benign, and messy: investment law against indigenous rights in Zimbabwe (the von Pezold arbitration), mainly non-instrumental with an instrumental streak, mainly cooperative with non-cooperative traits.

**Two inconsistencies in the paper**, noted for the author-note. (1) The conclusion says that roughly two-thirds of IICs are malign and one-third hybrid; the figures say 16 malign and 26 hybrid of 42, the other way round (p. 621 also says non-cooperative management is "dominant" among IICs, which holds only for the initial phase). The game follows the figures. (2) Table 1 gives 30 conflicts about interests and 52 across spheres; Figure 6's cells give 52 about interests (23+15+14) and 30 across spheres (0+22+8): the table's codes look reversed. The game uses neither variable.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**A mechanism made intelligible, which settles a quarrel.** One camp said overlapping institutions let states play them against each other, so complexity fragments global governance (Drezner; Benvenisti and Downs). The other said overlap brings flexibility and, in the long run, a division of labour (Keohane and Victor; Gehring and Faude). The paper: both are right, about different conflicts. What decides is *why* a clash began. Clashes that arise by accident are almost always settled together and mostly knit the institutions closer. Clashes started on purpose split: some are fought to the end and tear the order apart; more of them, surprisingly to the paper's own baseline, start as fights and end in cooperation, and the paper's best guess from its energy case is that this happens when the challenger's cause wins over a wider public.

## 4. The core narrative

The world has lots of clubs that make rules: one for money, one for refugees, one for energy. Sometimes two clubs' rules say opposite things about the same thing. Sometimes that happens by accident: something new turns up, and each club answers it its own way. Then the two clubs usually give a little each and end up working together better than before. But sometimes somebody makes two clubs clash on purpose, to knock one of them down. Those fights often end with everyone digging in, and the clubs drift apart. Unless the side that picked the fight has a cause that lots of people cheer for. Then the club that was attacked starts to bend, and the two end up partners.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The parties to a clash** (the institution whose rule is challenged, and its member states; the institution or coalition on the other side): they choose how to manage it, cooperatively ("follow procedural norms and accommodate each other's preferences at least somewhat") or not ("resolve the dispute solely in their favour"). Could be asked to anticipate: does the other side want a solution, or my institution's head? The player, in the pick.
- **The activator** (Netanyahu and Trump; the AU; the states that founded IRENA): chooses to create or revive a collision on purpose. Candidate agent; its aim is to win, and the order's coherence is collateral. Kept as the force that starts some clashes, drawn as an arrow.
- **The new problem** (a dam that floods villages, a war, a land reform): the thing that makes an accidental clash. Not an agent; drawn as a falling dot.
- **The wider audience** (trans- and international publics, other states): in the hybrid case its sympathy makes resisting costly. Candidate agent; not one in the paper. Kept as the crowd.
- **The coders** (six OSAIC teams, 86 items per case, 84% agreement on a double-coded subset) and **the analysts** (Gower and Ward): candidate experimenters. Concept B.
- **The rules themselves** (the norms in collision): candidate subject. Concept C.
- **Third parties** (ICSID arbitrators, the UN Secretary-General): appear in management; cut (section 14).

**What this game does differently.**

- **Position: an agent at the table, against a counterpart with a hidden motive.** Not an experimenter, not a builder, not a judge of what someone else did: the player is one of the two parties to each clash and decides how much of their own rulebook to rewrite to meet the other side. *I gave the Bank's page, they gave one back; I held against the bloc and kept my pages; I bent to the sun-and-wind agency once the crowd was behind it.*
- **Format: strategic, with hidden types and one purse.** Nine clashes, each one or two offers, fourteen pages for the whole game. The other side's behaviour follows from why the clash began; the player reads that, and spends. Curie's was a survey, Lovelace's a puzzle with a par, Noether's a deck of predictions; here the counterpart answers, and the crowd changes what holding out costs.
- **Device: the overprint (one of our own).** Two circles in two inks, the player's orange and theirs green; where they overlap, the clash, drawn as a lens of alternating orange and green stripes. A deal overprints the lens into a third colour, olive, with a row of stitches; a stand-off tears it white. Under the circles, the crowd gathers as a halftone of black dots, under one side or the other. No spot, no loom, no badge shelf.
- **Chassis: a Swiss International Style poster (Austere family: a Swiss grid poster).** After a comic on white (Drawn), a pasteboard instrument (Instrument) and a neon machine hall (Techno): flat inks on poster stock, a strict grid, one grotesk in lowercase, overlapping circles. Palette, type and buttons share nothing with the three.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**The aim** (start screen; top of the play screen; first line of How to play): *Settle as many of the nine clashes as you can. You have 14 pages to give, in all.*

The yardstick during play is in the aim's units: a row of nine marks (a mark turns olive the moment a clash is settled, white with a tear when it ends in a stand-off) and a column of fourteen pages that empties as the player gives them away or the crowd takes them. A settled clash is visibly a success at once; the tear is visibly a failure at once.

**The verdict** (end screen), three lines composed from what the player did, then the poster of the nine:

1. *What you did:* which clashes the player settled, by how they began, in words: e.g. "You settled all four that began by accident, for a page or two each. Of the five started on purpose, you settled the three where the crowd sided with the challenger, and the two that came to pull a body down tore, whatever you gave."
2. *What the paper says:* "Kreuder-Sonnen, Schmotz and Zürn coded 77 real clashes between international rulebooks, 1970 to 2018. Of the 35 that began by accident, 33 were settled together, and most knit the two bodies closer. Of the 42 started on purpose, 16 were fought to the end, and all but one tore the system apart; 26 were settled after all, most of them for good."
3. *What it means:* "So both old camps were right, about different clashes. Overlapping rulebooks pull the world together when they collide by accident, and apart when someone collides them on purpose, unless the one who picked the fight has the world on their side."

No score number: deals are counted on the row of marks during play; the verdict says what happened in words.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. Who Started It?** (agent: one of the two parties; strategic with hidden types and a budget).
- *Controls:* per offer, how many pages of your rulebook to rewrite to meet them: 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4 (five live options); MAKE THE OFFER. At most two offers per clash: the first answer and the last word.
- *Deliberation:* how did this clash begin (a new problem fell into the overlap, or someone pushed the other body into yours)? If on purpose: pay now, or hold and see whom the crowd backs, knowing that holding out against a popular cause costs pages too? And how many pages are left for what is still to come?
- *After:* the other side answers in the picture (they move to meet you and the lens overprints, or they take what you gave and push on); after a refused first offer, the crowd gathers under one side; years later, the seam is knit, frayed or torn; the ghost: what they would have settled for.
- *Moment:* the first clash started on purpose. The player, who has just settled an accident for one page, offers two; the other side takes them and pushes on; the crowd gathers under the player's own circle; no number of pages would have done. And then the next one started on purpose: the crowd gathers under *them*, holding out begins to cost, the player bends, and the lens overprints after all.
- *Fun:* anticipation (every offer waits on an answer), agency (pages are scarce and the player feels each one leave), discovery (twice: goodwill is wasted on a clash picked to pull you down; a clash picked on purpose can still end in a deal), mastery (by the sixth clash the player reads the origin and the crowd and spends exactly).
- *Second play:* a different deal from a pool of fourteen; the gamble the first play did not take (pay at once for a clash started on purpose); counting (the deck always holds the paper's proportions, so the last on-purpose clash can be read from the ones already seen).

**B. Seventy-Seven** (experimenter: the paper's analyst; discovery).
- *Controls:* 77 tiles, each a clash with its ending shown (knit or torn); five sieves (started on purpose? about power and money or about values? inside one field or across fields? did one side gain more? handled together?), three of which may be run; each sieve drops the tiles into two trays.
- *Deliberation:* which question do I think separates the clashes that held from those that broke?
- *Moment:* the money-or-values sieve, which everyone expects to matter, leaves both trays equally torn; the origin sieve sorts the management almost perfectly.
- *Fun:* discovery, a little anticipation. *Second play:* little.
- *Why it loses:* the case-level codes are not in the paper and the dataset was not attached; the tiles would be our reconstruction from marginals, with joint distributions invented, and the sieve results would be our assumptions displayed as data. And it is Curie's position and format again (the experimenter, a survey under a budget).

**C. Push** (subject: the player is a rule; real time, no words on the play screen; **the reckless one**).
- *Controls:* a thumb on the edge of your shape where it meets another. Press to hold your ground, ease to give it.
- *Deliberation:* none in words; a feel for how the other shape answers.
- *Moment:* one neighbour eases when you ease and the overlap stitches; another, which arrived behind a black arrow, shoves harder whenever you ease, and the overlap grinds and tears; a third shoves with a flood of dots at its back, your shape fades while you hold, and when you let go the overlap stitches.
- *Fun:* agency through the thumb, discovery. *Second play:* a steadier thumb.
- *Why it is reckless:* no words, real time, an abstract rule as the agent. It loses the reading of the origin, which is the paper's variable, to reflex, and the hybrid path looks like losing.

## 9. The first five minutes

**A (the pick).** *0:00.* A poster: two big circles, orange and green, overlapping; the lens between them striped orange and green. Lowercase, very large: *who started it?* Under it: "The world's rulebooks overlap. Where two say opposite things, they clash." The aim on its own line. I tap BEGIN.

*0:15.* Clash 1 of 9. My circle (orange) is the Development Bank; my rule: no politics in our loans. Theirs (green) is the Rights Office; their rule: people driven off their land by a project have rights. A black dot falls into the lens and lands there: "a dam that will flood villages". One line: nobody planned this clash; a new problem landed where both rulebooks apply. They say: "Let's find a way through this together." Under the picture: *Rewrite how many pages of your rulebook to meet them?* 0 to 4, and a column of fourteen pages at the right. I touch 1: the border in the lens slides a notch into my circle and one page lifts from my column. I press MAKE THE OFFER. "You rewrote 1 page. They rewrote one of theirs: a deal." The lens overprints olive and a row of stitches runs across it. Years later: knit, the Bank checks rights before it lends. Under it: "They would have settled for 1 page." Easy.

*1:00.* Clash 2. The Council; theirs, the Regional Bloc. No dot: a fat black arrow shoves the green circle into mine. "Started on purpose: a bloc that wants your Council out of its region dug up an old rule of non-interference." They say: "Your rule stops at our border." I think: last time one page did it; this one is angrier, so two. "You rewrote 2 pages. They took them and pushed on: no deal." Then dots gather, under *my* circle. "The crowd: other governments, the press, people far away. This one is with you: the bloc's cause wins few friends outside its camp. Holding out costs you nothing." Last word: I give nothing. Stand-off: the lens tears white. "They would not have settled for any number of pages: they wanted your Council out." I frown: two pages for nothing. Twelve left.

*2:00.* Clash 3. The Oil Agency; theirs, the Sun and Wind Agency, and the arrow again: founded on purpose by countries that want the world off oil, to challenge me. "Your rules must give way to ours." I have learnt: 0. "No deal." Dots gather, under *them*, a lot of dots. "This crowd is with them: their cause is popular. Holding out against it has cost you a page already (your own members are drifting to them), and will cost you two more if you end without a deal." I weigh it: twelve pages, eleven now; bend or hold? I give 2. "They take them, and sign: a deal, after a fight." The lens overprints. The ghost: "They'd have settled for 2, at the very start too." So a fight picked on purpose can end in a deal: when the world is with the one who picked it. I grin.

*3:00 to 5:00.* Clash 4, a dot: one page, a deal. Clash 5: a dot *and* an arrow, a government glad to use a new problem; I give one, no deal, no crowd; one more, a deal. Clash 6, an arrow: now I hold first; the crowd goes to them; I bend for two. Clash 7, an arrow: I hold; the crowd comes to me; I hold again; it tears, and I don't mind. Clash 8, a dot: one page. Clash 9, an arrow, and I have three pages: hold, the crowd goes to them and takes one, I give my last two: a deal, with nothing left. *5:30.* The poster of my nine, the three lines, and the paper's 77 as three heaps of dots beside my nine.

**C (the runner-up, and the reckless one).** *0:00.* Two shapes touching, an orange and a green, and the words *you are a rule. hold.* *0:10.* My thumb on my edge. I press; the green eases; I ease; it eases further; the overlap stitches. Pleasant, unexplained. *0:40.* A green shape slides in behind a black arrow. I press; it shoves; I ease; it shoves harder; the overlap grinds and tears. I do not know whether I did something wrong. *1:20.* Another arrow, and dots flood in behind the green. I hold; my shape fades; I hold harder; it fades faster; I let go and the overlap stitches. Did I lose? Did I win? *2:30.* I have felt three kinds of neighbour and cannot name them. Without words the player cannot know that the arrow means *on purpose* or the dots a public, and the paper's sentence is out of reach: "and I don't know what I'm supposed to feel".

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, Who Started It?** The player is inside the paper's model at the place it puts agency: a party managing a clash. The origin is the hidden truth the player must read, as the paper's coders had to; the management is what the player does; the order effect is what follows. The discovery lands inside the first two minutes (pages given to a clash picked to pull you down are lost; the crowd sits with you, and holding out is free) and its twist inside three (the crowd behind a challenger makes holding out cost, and the clash picked on purpose ends in a deal). The deliberation is real every time: pay now or wait, how much, and what to keep for later. The sentence afterwards has no "guess": *I settled the accidents for a page each, held against the bloc that wanted my Council out, and bent to the sun-and-wind agency once the crowd was behind it.* Originality: no game in the table has put the player across a table from a counterpart whose motive is hidden, or drawn regime complexity as overprinted inks.

**Author test.** The authors would recognise their Figure 2 run forwards (origin, management, effect), their coordination games (a page given is a page returned) and collaboration games (a page given is a page taken), the "at least initially" of IIC management, their IRENA explanation turned into a mechanism, their three clusters in exact proportion in every deck, the Zimbabwe case as the messy one, and their 77 on the last screen. A colleague who knows the answer still has to spend fourteen pages well against a deck that does not say which challenger is which until the crowd forms, and may enjoy discovering that their own hybrid cluster is the one that eats the budget.

**Runner-up: C, Push.** Switch if the authors want a ninety-second toy for a keynote, in which the three paths are felt rather than read, and are willing to put the three origins in words on the start screen.

**What the reckless concept would have given.** The other side's motive felt through the thumb, without a word. Kept from it: the other side's answer is drawn as motion in the picture (they move to meet you, or they push on), and the result line in words comes after the motion. Given up: the silence and the real time.

## 11. The shape

One game of nine clashes, each one or two offers: about five to six minutes. Screens: the poster (start); the clash (play; one per clash, the same layout); the end poster (verdict); How to play; About. No presets: the paper has no regimes to switch; its three clusters are in every deck, in the paper's proportions (35 : 26 : 16 is 4.1 : 3.0 : 1.9 in nine): four that began by accident (one of them messy, after the Zimbabwe case), three started on purpose with a popular cause (hybrid), two started on purpose to pull a body down (malign). Ordered for the player: clash 1 is an accident (the basic deal); clashes 2 and 3 are one of each kind started on purpose, in random order (the discovery and its twist, both inside three minutes); clashes 4 to 9 are the rest, shuffled. Each deck is drawn from a pool of fourteen clashes. Score: none shown; the row of marks and the column of pages are the yardstick; the verdict is in words. The game ends once; PLAY AGAIN deals a new deck.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls:** on the clash screen, five page buttons (0 to 4) in a row and MAKE THE OFFER. Every offer starts at 0. Touching a number previews it in the picture: the border inside the lens slides that many notches into your circle, and that many pages lift from your column. Nothing in the preview says whether they will take it.
- **Mechanism (formalised from the argument; numbers in CONFIG):** each clash has a hidden kind. *Accident* (benign): settles when the pages you have given reach 1; if your first answer is 0, they dig in too and the price becomes 2. They rewrite a page of their own when they settle. *Messy* (the Zimbabwe case): settles at 2; dig in to 3 if your first answer is 0. *On purpose, popular* (hybrid): settles at 2, in either offer; if the first offer does not settle it, the crowd gathers behind them and takes 1 page from you; if the clash ends without a deal, it takes 2 more. *On purpose, to pull you down* (malign): no price; pages given are taken and lost; after the first offer the crowd gathers behind you and takes nothing. No deal at the end is a stand-off. Years later, a deal knits the seam with probability 0.76 (accident; 25 of 33 cooperative NIICs) or 0.77 (on purpose; 20 of 26), otherwise it frays; a stand-off tears with probability 0.94 (15 of 16), otherwise it knits after all. A "historical" player (pays the price at once to accidents; holds, then bends to popular challengers; holds against the rest) reproduces the paper's clusters: 4 of 4 accidents settled (paper 33 of 35), 3 of 5 on-purpose clashes settled after a fight (paper 26 of 42), the knit rates by draw. Pages: 14, which is exactly what that historical player spends; a player who reads the deck perfectly from the first offer would spend 11.
- **Feedback:** the falling dot or the shoving arrow (how it began); after an offer, the other circle moving: toward yours, rewriting its own page, then the lens overprinting olive with stitches; or pushing on, with your given pages flying across into the green; the crowd gathering as halftone dots under one circle, with pages flying from your column if it is against you; years later, the seam knit, frayed or torn; the ghost price under the result.
- **Moment:** the first clash picked on purpose (goodwill lost, the crowd with you), and the second (the crowd against you, the bend, the overprint).

## 13. The look

**Register: a Swiss International Style poster** (Austere family: a Swiss grid poster). It comes from the setting of the data and from the paper's object at once. The institutions in the ICD live largely in Geneva and its neighbours (the UN's human-rights office, UNHCR, WHO, the WTO, ILO), and the style that was Switzerland's house style from the 1950s to the 1970s, when the dataset begins, is the one that called itself *international*. Its signature image, flat transparent inks in overlapping circles (Müller-Brockmann's concert posters), is the paper's object: overlapping institutions. The drawing is the mechanism: circles are bodies, the lens is the overlap, the overprint is integration.

- **Palette:** poster stock `#EAE6DC` (the ground; a tear shows it); ink `#141414` (type, the arrow, the falling dot, the crowd, the primary button); signal orange `#F2581B` (your rule: your circle, your pages, your border); grass green `#1C9A55` (their rule: their circle, their border); olive `#55601A` (the overprint of the two: a deal, and nothing else).
- **Type:** Archivo (Omnibus-Type, SIL OFL), a grotesk in the Akzidenz manner, one family throughout: 800 for the lowercase headlines and the big numerals, 600 for buttons and labels, 400 for text. Embedded as data.
- **Buttons:** flat slabs on the grid, square corners, no border, no shadow; lowercase label flush left and an arrow flush right; the primary action is an ink slab with stock-coloured lettering; the page buttons are 56 px squares with a 2 px ink rule, numbered in 800, filled orange when chosen.
- **The one visual rule:** two inks for two rules; they mix only where a deal is made. Everything orange is yours, everything green is theirs, and olive, their overprint, appears nowhere except in a settled seam and in the mark that counts it.
- **Play screen at 380 px:** the aim (two lines, 15 px) with ? at the right; a strip with "clash 3 of 9", the row of nine marks (14 px squares: empty, olive, torn) and the column of fourteen pages turned on its side as a row of 14 orange slabs (answers "how am I doing, and what have I got left?"); the picture, 348 × 250: two circles of 150 px radius overlapping by about 90 px, the lens striped, each circle's name inside it in 800, its rule in 400 under the name, the dot or the arrow arriving, and the crowd band under the circles (answers "what is this clash, how did it begin, and who is the world with?"); the story line in the game's voice (what happened, 2 lines) and what they say (1 line in quotes, green rule at left); the offer row: the question, the five page buttons, one line of what a page does; MAKE THE OFFER. After an offer, in the place of the offer row: the result in the game's voice (2 lines), then the crowd line if any, then the last word or NEXT CLASH.
- **End screen:** the nine clashes as a Swiss poster: nine small circle pairs on a 3 × 3 grid, each lens olive, frayed or torn, and the heading in 800 lowercase; the three verdict lines; the paper's 77 as three blocks of dots (35, 26, 16), each dot olive (knit) or open (torn), with the player's nine placed beside them; PLAY AGAIN; How to play; About.

## 14. What is cut

- **Object, spheres and outcome** (the extended cluster variables). The spheres finding (hybrid clashes cross spheres of authority) is the paper's own open puzzle; it is named on About, not played.
- **Third parties.** Arbitration, the Secretary-General's appeals, the courts. Management is played as two parties; the Zimbabwe arbitration survives only as the messy clash's story.
- **Real names in play.** The clashes are made up, each in the shape of a kind of case in the paper; the paper's own three cases (UNRWA and UNHCR, IEA and IRENA, Zimbabwe) and its two examples (World Bank and OHCHR, AU and UN Security Council) are told on the end screen and About. A player who settles a clash modelled on the UNRWA case would be rewriting a real refugee agency's mandate; the game declines to make that a move.
- **The activator's seat.** Playing the one who picks the fight (concept "Pick a fight", considered and dropped) would play the hybrid and malign clusters and leave the benign one, the paper's main finding, outside the game.
- **The dendrograms and the clustering method.** On About, in a sentence.

## 15. Divergences

- **The clashes are fictions** in the shapes of the paper's cases, not records from the ICD. Reason: no case-level data in the paper, and real cases with known endings would be a quiz for the authors.
- **Pages, prices and the purse are ours.** The paper has cooperative and non-cooperative management, not a currency. Accident 1, messy 2, popular challenger 2, hostile challenger none; 14 in all. Reason: a concession has to cost something, or giving everything to everyone is the answer.
- **The crowd is our mechanism for the hybrid path.** The paper's explanation comes from one case (IRENA) and is offered as a first insight, with the conditions called an open question; the game makes every hybrid clash run through it, and makes every malign clash's crowd side with the challenged body (as the General Assembly did with UNRWA). The cost of holding out against a popular cause (pages taken) is our reading of "a legitimacy crisis". Declared on About.
- **Accidents dig in if met with nothing.** Ours; the paper has NIICs that turn non-cooperative "for idiosyncratic reasons" (2 of 35), which are otherwise dropped: an accident met with its price always settles.
- **Two offers stand for years of management.** The paper's IEA–IRENA sequence ran from 2009 to 2019.
- **The deck's proportions are fixed** at 4 : 3 : 2 (the clusters' 35 : 26 : 16) rather than drawn, so every game shows all three paths.
- **The order effect is drawn** with the clusters' rates; the paper treats each arrow as "the most likely consequence", which is what the draw does.
- **The counts on the end screen are the paper's.** The figures' count of malign against hybrid (16 against 26) is used, not the conclusion's "two-thirds malign".

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Kind: a toy, calibrated to the paper's three clusters.** The paper hands over an argument and a cluster analysis, not a model; the game formalises the argument as a bargaining toy in which the player is one party and the other party's behaviour is fixed by the clash's origin, and calibrates the toy's deck and its "years later" draws to Figure 4.

- **Demonstrates**, under the game's assumptions: that the argument can work as described. The same goodwill settles an accident cheaply, is lost on a challenger who wants the body gone, and pays off with a challenger the crowd backs; a player who behaves as the paper's actors do reproduces the paper's counts (section 17).
- **Assumes**: pages, prices and the purse; that accidents dig in when met with nothing; the crowd and what it costs; two offers for years of management; the made-up clashes; the fixed 4 : 3 : 2 deck (section 15).
- **The paper establishes**: three clusters in 77 coded conflicts, and the sequence origin, management, effect traced in three case studies. It says that cluster analysis does not test causes, that the sample is not random, and that motives are hard to see and often mixed. The game's outcomes are no evidence for its claims; the About screen says so.

**Where each staged discovery comes from.**

1. *Accidents settle, for little.* The paper (benign cluster: 33 of 35 cooperative). The price of one page is ours.
2. *Goodwill is lost on a clash started to pull a body down.* The paper (malign cluster: 0 of 16 cooperative; the UNRWA case, where the challengers wanted the agency abolished and "none of the various actors involved seems to be willing to compromise"). That the pages are *lost* is ours.
3. *A clash started on purpose can end in a deal when the crowd backs the challenger.* The cluster is the paper's (26 of 42 IICs cooperative); the crowd as its cause is the paper's guess from one case (IRENA), generalised by us to every hybrid clash. Because this rests on an assumption the paper does not identify, the game says so at the moment of discovery: the grey line under every such clash reads "its guess, from one case, is that the challenger's cause had won over a wider public", and About repeats it.
4. *Waiting for the crowd beats paying at once.* A game mechanic, from our prices and crowd costs (section 18). Nothing on screen presents it as the paper's.

**Robustness** (calibration.js, section D). The central discovery (reading how a clash began settles at least as many clashes as any strategy blind to it, and loses fewer pages to clashes nobody can settle; with pages no object, every accident and every popular challenge can be settled and no hostile one) survives: popular challengers asking 3 pages; accidents asking 2 (messy 3); a crowd that takes nothing; a crowd that takes 2, then 3; purses of 11 and 18; and a crowd that sides the wrong way one time in five. One honest limit: with a purse of 18, blind generosity ("meet halfway") also settles all seven; the reader still loses no pages to the hostile challengers where the blind player loses two. The purse is what makes reading matter *for the count*; who can be settled at all does not depend on it.

## 17. Calibration plan and result

**Plan.** (A) The draw, over 2000 decks: composition, the floor (clash 1 an accident; 2 and 3 one of each kind started on purpose), reproducibility by deck number, uniformity of the years-later draws. (B) The paper reproduced by a "historical" player who behaves as the paper's actors do (meets accidents at once; holds against a clash started on purpose, then gives way if the crowd backs the challenger): origin share against Table 1 (54.5 %), cooperative share (76.6 %), integrative share (60.5 %), each cluster's cooperation and integration against Figure 4, the cooperative-but-fragmenting sub-clusters against note 10; tolerance 2 percentage points (3 for the hybrid share of IICs, 4 for the benign integrative rate overall, which is higher by the declared drop of the two idiosyncratic stand-offs). (C) The obvious strategies. (D) Robustness. (E) The rules: pages never negative, offers capped, hostile never settles, settlement exactly at the price, crowd only after a first offer that fails, dig-in.

**Result: 48 of 48 pass** (`calibration-result.txt`). Origin 55.6 % on purpose (paper 54.5); cooperative 77.8 % (76.6); integrative 61.2 % (60.5); benign cooperative 100 % (94.3, declared), integrative when settled 76.5 % (75.8); hybrid share of IICs 60.0 % (61.9), integrative 77.4 % (76.9); malign cooperative 0 (0), integrative 6.2 % (6.3); cooperative but fragmenting 23.4 % benign (24.2) and 22.6 % hybrid (23.1). The historical player settles all seven settleable clashes in every one of 2000 decks and spends exactly 14 pages.

The spread is wide (0 to 7), so decisions matter. One simple rule wins everywhere, and it is the sentence the player should leave with: *settle the accidents at once; with a clash started on purpose, see whom the world backs.* It is also the paper's historical behaviour, which is why the purse is set to what it costs (14). The gambler comes close (6.2): paying at once is a fair bet, which keeps the first offer on a clash started on purpose a real decision. A first-time learner (one page to the first accident, two to the first clash started on purpose, then the rule) settles six of seven in the scripted sessions.
