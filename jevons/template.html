Noon

It is 1865. Britain runs on coal: its mills, its engines, its ships. Every year it burns more than the year before. The dotted line is what happens if that goes on.

All the coal Britain has is in the ground, drawn here as black squares. Burn it fast and it makes a big fire that runs out. Burn it slow and it makes a small fire that lasts. You decide the pace, one generation at a time.

Choose for Britain: a brief greatness, or a long mediocrity.

How to play

Choose for Britain: a brief greatness, or a long mediocrity.

The sheet

It is graph paper, ruled like Jevons's own. The years run along the black line in the middle, from 1850 to 2070.

Above the line is Britain's fire: how much coal it burns in a year. The higher the curve, the bigger the fire. The black under the curve is coal that has been burnt, and you can count it in squares.

Below the line is the coal still in the ground: 167 black squares, in eight layers, down to 4,000 feet. One square below is the same amount of coal as one square above. When a square is burnt above, a square goes from below. The top layer is dug first. The deeper the digging, the dearer the coal.

The dashed line is America's coal. The hatched bar at the bottom is people who went over the sea because there was no work for them. Orange is whatever is burning now. If the fire grows taller than the paper, more paper is added at the top.

Each generation

You set six turns of twenty years, from 1865 to 1985. Slide along the ruler under the sheet to say how much coal Britain should burn twenty years from now: cut back (half as much), a stand (the same), slower (half as much again), as now (twice as much: Jevons's own pace in 1865) or faster. Under each mark the ruler says how long the coal would last at that pace. The dotted line on the sheet is your plan, and the crossed squares are the coal it would take. Then press Burn. The solid line is what actually burnt, and it may leave your dotted line.

After your sixth turn the sheet runs on by itself to 2065, at the last pace you set, so that you can see where your fire ends up.

The engines

In 1885 and 1925 an engine is offered that does the same work on less coal. Fit it, or refuse it. America fits every new engine at once.

The evening

If coal has grown so dear that the fire could not grow at any pace, the evening has come: the fire falls whatever you set, and the rest of the sheet draws itself.

The end

There is no score. The last page reads your sheet: how high the fire went, and how long it lasted. Then the curve, continued.
