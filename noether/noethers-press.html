A bead moves in a small world. You can change the whole world in six ways: start its clock later, slide it, turn it, or re-tick its clock any way you draw with your thumb.

Some changes the world will not notice. Each of those is worth a law: something that never changes while the bead moves. A press stamps the law on a steel tag. An auditor then throws fake motions at the tag to see if it can catch them.

Four worlds, three blank tags each. A change the world notices wastes a tag. About six minutes.

THE SHIFT IS OVER

    What the paper says

    A symmetry with a parameter in it stamps a law. Start the clock later, shift the world, turn it: each one the world does not notice gives a quantity that holds steady on every true motion and on nothing else. That is Theorem I, and it runs backwards: a law with teeth points to a symmetry.

    A symmetry with a free hand in it, one you can apply differently at every moment, stamps no law. It stamps an identity: the equations of motion are not all independent, and as many of them as there are free hands follow from the rest. That is Theorem II, and it also runs backwards.

    Now put the two together. If the free hand contains the shift of the clock, the clock's law is inside the identity: it holds for every motion, fake or true, and says nothing. The paper calls such a law improper. That is the answer to Hilbert's question. In Einstein's theory of gravity the coordinates can be relabelled freely, moment by moment; the shift of the time coordinate is inside that freedom; so the energy law it yields is empty. Energy is not lost. There is no clock in the theory to keep it by.

    The paper's last word: Hilbert said this failure is characteristic of general relativity. It is, exactly when "general relativity" means invariance under a group with free hands in it; and it holds for any such theory, in any number of dimensions.

    Both ways

    A tag with teeth means a symmetry with a parameter. A blank tag means a free hand that contains the shift you pulled. Either way, the world and its laws are the same thing seen from two sides.

    How to play

    Find the changes this world does not notice.
    Each one stamps a law.

    The world. The window shows a bead moving in one small world: on a flat floor, on a spring, as light in glass, on a stretched sheet. The line above the window says what the world is and what it never does. The white trail is a real motion, something this world really does. The dots on the trail are the ticks of the bead's clock: one dot per tick.

    The levers. Each lever changes the whole world in one way. Tap one and the window shows the change in blue, with the real motion faint and grey underneath. Four levers make a fixed change: start the clock a moment later, slide the world sideways or up, turn it. The two magenta levers make a change you draw with your thumb: re-tick the clock faster here and slower there, or push the world sideways by a different amount at every moment.

    The question. Would this world run just the same after the change? A bead that nothing pushes would not mind being slid sideways. It would mind a clock that runs fast and slow, because then it speeds up and slows down for no reason. If you think the world would not notice, PULL.

    The press. If the world does not notice, the press holds and stamps a steel tag with the law that follows: one quantity that will now stay fixed on every real motion. If the world does notice, the press jams, the blank bends, and you lose it. Three blanks per world; every pull uses one.

    The Auditor and the needle. He tests every tag. He plays real motions (white) and fakes (magenta, dashed): motions this world would never make. The needle reads the tag's quantity as the bead moves. Still on the real motions and swinging on a fake: a law with TEETH, cyan. It can tell a fake from the real thing. Still on everything, the fakes too: VOID, magenta. A tag that no fake can break rules nothing out; it is empty.

    The drawn levers. When the press holds for a change you drew, no law comes out. The tag reads IDENTITY: a rule about the equations of motion themselves, that they are not independent. Watch what that does to the clock tag in that world.

    The Auditor moves first. In two worlds he already holds a law and asks which lever it comes from, or holds a blank tag and asks which lever explains it. Pull the lever you think it is; a wrong pull still uses a blank.

    The end. After four worlds the verdict is in words: what you stamped, what came out blank, and what the paper says it means.

    About

    The game in one line

    A press stamps a law for every change a world does not notice; an inspector feeds fakes to every tag; and when you re-tick the clock freely in a world that lets you, the clock's tag comes out blank. That blank is Hilbert's answer.

    What the paper does

    Emmy Noether's 1918 paper is about a variational integral (an action) that is invariant under a continuous group of transformations, and it proves two theorems. Theorem I: if the group has ρ parameters, there are ρ independent combinations of the Lagrange expressions (the equations of motion) that are divergences, which is to say ρ conservation laws; and conversely, ρ conservation laws imply invariance under such a group. Theorem II: if the group depends on ρ arbitrary functions and their derivatives, there are ρ identities between the Lagrange expressions and their derivatives, so that ρ of the equations of motion are consequences of the rest; and conversely. Her first example of Theorem II is the Weierstrass parametric integral, invariant under any re-labelling of the parameter, with the identity Σψᵢuᵢ' = 0.

    Section 6 answers a question of Hilbert's: why do proper energy theorems fail in general relativity? Because when a finite group (the shifts of the independent variables) sits inside an infinite group under which the integral is invariant, the conservation laws of the finite group become improper: linear combinations of the identities of Theorem II, plus divergences that vanish identically; they hold whether or not the equations of motion do. Proper energy theorems exist exactly when there is no such infinite group (classical mechanics, special relativity). Hilbert's claim that this failure is characteristic of general relativity holds, she ends, only if "general relativity" is taken to mean invariance under a group depending on arbitrary functions.

    Mechanism: ported

    This game demonstrates the theorems' identities on four small worlds, computed, not quoted: for every lever the Lie condition (her equation 11) is checked on random paths; a lever that passes gives the current B of her equations 12 and 13, which the Auditor reads along true motions (solutions of the equations of motion) and fakes (bumped and re-ticked paths); a free-hand lever that passes gives the identity of her equation 16, checked on random paths. On the glass and the sheet (integrals homogeneous of first degree in the velocities, her example on p. 7) the clock's current is identically zero: improper, as section 6 says; on the floor and the spring it is the energy, and proper.

    It assumes the worlds: two Newton-type integrals (a free bead; a bead on a spring) and two Weierstrass-type integrals (light in glass whose density depends on depth, which is Fermat's principle; a bead on a sheet with a well, a geodesic in a conformally flat metric), with profiles chosen for a visible bend. One parameter instead of four coordinates: the "free hand" here is the re-ticking of a single clock, Noether's own simplest case, and Einstein's group of all coordinate transformations is played as that. Six levers, three blanks, an Auditor with seven test motions, thresholds for "steady" and "swings": all ours, in the config block at the top of the source with their reasons. The tags' names are what the quantities measure; their formulas are in the source.

    The paper establishes the two theorems and their converses for any number of variables and derivatives, and the section 6 result on proper and improper energy relations. It does not say what energy "is" in general relativity, and neither does the game; it says that the law attached to the shift of a coordinate is an identity there. The world of the sheet is a one-dimensional picture of that, not a model of gravity.

    What is cut

    Higher derivatives and the n-fold integral; mixed groups; the construction of finite transformations from infinitesimal ones (her sections 3 and 4); section 5 on relative invariance; the nonlinear dependencies between first integrals; Einstein's modified integral I* and the exchange with Klein in the notes. The group structure itself: the player pulls one infinitesimal transformation at a time.
