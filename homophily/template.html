THE HOME BONUS

Every judge card shows two facts: the judge's passport and the country the judge works in.

The study found that a judge gives the very same proposal a better mark when the applicant has the same passport and works in the same country: about a sixth of a point on a 1–5 scale. Sharing only one of the two gives roughly a third to a half as much. Same gender: about a seventh. Too small to show here.

Each proposal is read by about 4 of the 15 judges, picked at random. The four bars sort applicants by their passport and where they work. Each bar shows the home advantage that group collects on average, counted in home stars per 10 juries. One home star is one judge who shares both passport and work country with the applicant.

HOW DO THEY KNOW IT'S NOT JUST BETTER PROPOSALS?

The researchers compared the same proposal as marked by its different judges, so quality is held fixed. They also compared each judge with their own marks for other applicants, so a generous judge is not mistaken for a biased one. The extra marks are top marks, not a wider spread, so it does not look like inside knowledge. And the bonus stays when they account for judges who work on the same topics or have written papers with the same people.

The study calls it homophily: favouring people like yourself. It does not say whether anyone means to.

HOW TO PLAY
You choose the judges for a big science funder.

The European Research Council gives out big grants. Each research field has a panel of about 15 judges, and each proposal is marked by about four of them.

Judge cards. Each card says the judge's passport and the country the judge works in, for example PASSPORT DE, WORKS IN UK: a German who works in the UK. The colours only help the eye: yellow for the Big Five (Germany, the UK, France, Italy, Spain), teal for other countries, lilac for outside Europe.

The home bonus. The study found that a judge gives the same proposal about a sixth of a point more (marks run 1–5) when the applicant has the same passport and works in the same country as the judge. That is one home star. The same passport only is worth about half a star; the same work country only, about a third. "Same" means the same named country: a German and a French judge are not the same just because both countries are in the Big Five.

The four bars sort applicants, never judges: by passport (Big Five or other) and by where they work (in the Big Five or elsewhere). Each bar is that group's average home advantage, in home stars per 10 juries, weighted by how many applicants are in it.

The target. Under the bars: all applicants who work in the Big Five (both passport rows together, weighted by size), all who work elsewhere, and the gap between them. The four bars do not have to be equal.

  • Round 1: tap judges in the pool to seat them; tap a seated judge to send them back. When the 15th judge is seated, the bars are uncovered.
  • Round 2: a tilted panel built like the ERC's. Tap a judge at the table, then one on the bench, to swap them. Get the gap to half a star or less in four swaps or fewer. RESET starts the round again.

Tap any judge to see whom they favour, and how many applicants that is.
