WEIL'S VASE

The Poem of Force

Force turns whoever it touches into a thing.

You are the soul inside a warrior. Each day of a war, a man comes before you. While Zeus's scales tip your way, you decide what to do with him; when they turn, a man stands over you.

Force is yours, for now.
Keep your soul through the war.

How to play

Force is yours, for now. Keep your soul through the war.

What you see

You are the soul inside the warrior on the left of the vase. Your figure is clay while it has a soul: six fine lines, at your knees, thighs, belly, chest, mouth and eye. When you lose one, stone climbs from your feet: black glaze, with only its outline scratched in. A figure that is stone all through is a thing.

Above the figures hang Zeus's golden scales, your side marked YOURS. While they tip your way, force is yours that day: a man comes before you and you decide what to do with him. When they tip against you, force is his: a man stands over you. The scales turn by themselves, day by day; what you do with force only holds the turn off a little, or brings it nearer, and sets how far they swing.

What you can do

  • Strike: kill him (or beat him down).
  • Take: take him, or her, alive, as a slave or a prize.
  • Pass: go on as if he were not there.
  • Raise: give him your hand.

One touch on a sherd does the thing; the three words under it say what. A sherd shown cracked cannot be played on this man; it says why.

When the scales tip against you: Plead (clasp his knees), Obey (be his, and live) or Stand (face him). A man who is still clay can hear a plea; a man of stone cannot.

What to watch for

An armed man who is nearly all stone will strike if you do not. When the scales stand far your way, or when little of your soul is left, your arm starts to move toward him by itself: touch a sherd before it reaches him, or the spear goes in. At the very top, or with no soul left, there is no interval at all.

Twelve days, the last a night. A life ends on the night, or earlier if you die or become a man's slave. The vase is then turned round, and you see every day of it.

About

the game in one line

You are the soul inside a warrior on an Attic vase. For twelve days a man comes before you, and while Zeus's scales tip your way you decide what to do with him: strike, take, pass, or raise him up. Whatever force you use, or suffer, turns a little more of you to stone; and the scales swing back.

what the essay says

Simone Weil wrote The Iliad, or the Poem of Force in the summer and autumn of 1940, after the fall of France. Its first sentence names the poem's true hero: force, “that x that turns anybody who is subjected to it into a thing”: a corpse, or, more strangely, a man still alive who has become a thing, the suppliant, the slave. Nobody in the poem possesses force: “there is not a single man who does not at one time or another have to bow his neck” to it, and victory is blind destiny, “symbolized in the poem by Zeus's golden scales”. The man who holds force walks through a world that offers no resistance, so nothing interposes “between the impulse and the act, the tiny interval that is reflection”; he exceeds the measure of a force he does not know is limited, and a retribution “which has a geometrical rigor” comes home to him. A moderate use of force “would require superhuman virtue”, and moderation has its own perils, since prestige, “from which force derives at least three quarters of its strength”, rests on the indifference of the strong. And the edge cuts both ways: “to the same degree, though in different fashions, those who use it and those who endure it are turned to stone.” Against all this stand a few moments of grace, rare and brief: enemies who turn their lances away from each other, Priam and Achilles looking at each other in the night. The essay's last pages read the poem's equity, its bitterness for victor and vanquished alike, as the Greek genius, carried on in tragedy and the Gospels and lost since.

mechanism: a toy

The essay is an argument, with no model and no data, so the game runs a toy built to illustrate it. Your soul is six lines, lost from the feet up. Every act of force you use (strike, take) costs one; every act of force you suffer (struck by a man of stone, kneeling) costs one; passing or raising a man costs none. Zeus's scales are a seesaw: fate turns them on its own clock, and how far they swing is three quarters prestige (what you do with force) and one quarter fate. Force used today holds the turn off a little, and widens the swing; mercy brings the turn sooner, and narrows it. When the scales tip against you, the man over you stands as high as the scales are swinging, and the higher a man stands, the more of him is stone; a plea is heard in proportion to the clay left in him. When the scales stand far your way, or little of your soul is left, the interval goes: your arm moves by itself, and at the top it moves at once. Grace gives a line back to each man.

This game demonstrates, under its own assumptions, that Weil's argument can work as she describes it: played over many lives, a player who strikes everything ends as stone and usually dies on his knees under a man as deaf as his own swing made him; a player who spares everyone kneels more often; a player who uses force only against the man who would strike him keeps the most of his soul and still dies before the night in about a third of lives; nobody who lives nine days escapes kneeling. These patterns hold when the toy's own numbers are moved by a quarter or more either way, except that a scale that turns much more slowly lets a hard player stay on top for all twelve days.

It assumes (all ours): that a soul has six lines and that each act of force, used or suffered, costs exactly one; the seesaw and its numbers (how fast fate turns it, how much a gesture pushes it, how much it holds the turn off); that the man over you is as stony as the scales are high, and hears a plea in proportion to his clay; that an armed man nearly all stone strikes unless you strike first, and one half stone strikes if you have spared enough men that he does not fear you; that standing to face him lets you live one time in four (fate's quarter); the timer of the interval; the twelve days. The men are made up, each in the shape of a scene the essay quotes. Weil's “three quarters” and Lycaon's “twelfth day” are hers; every other number is in the config block at the top of the source, with its reason.

The essay establishes a reading of the Iliad, argued from its text: what force does to men, in the poem, on both sides. It is not an empirical claim about how often men die or are spared, and the game's deaths, pleas and swings are no evidence for it.

what is cut

The essay's last third (the Greek genius, tragedy and the Gospels, Rome and the Hebrews, the chansons de geste, Racine) is read, not played. The world of peace (“far from hot baths”), the gods who kindle men's madness, the slave's inner life, the similes that make men into fire and flood, and the twelve Trojan boys on Patroclus' pyre are not scenes. The equity of the poem is the end screen's point of view: every man you met, and you, as you ended.
