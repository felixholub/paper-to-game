What the paper says

Noether's paper is about formulas that give every path a number, where nature takes the path with the smallest one. She proved two things.

First: whenever the formula doesn't care about a change, such as moving the picture, turning it, or starting the clock later, there is a number that stays the same all along nature's path, and on no other path. That is where energy, momentum and spin come from. It works backwards too: every such number comes from a change the formula doesn't care about.

Second: when the formula doesn't care about a much bigger change, one you can make differently at every moment, like re-ticking the clock fast here and slow there, there is no such number. You get a rule instead: the equations of motion are not independent. And any prize that sits inside the bigger change becomes empty. It stays the same on every path, the impossible ones too, so it tells you nothing.

That answers a question Hilbert had asked. Einstein's theory of gravity is a formula of the second kind: you may relabel space and time freely, moment by moment, and it doesn't care. So its energy is like the slide's: empty. That is why energy seems to go missing in Einstein's theory. Nothing is lost; the theory has no clock of its own to count energy with.

How to play

Watch a change. Say whether it matters.
Every change that doesn't matter wins a prize.

The formula. Each picture has a formula, shown on the plate in symbols and in words. It gives every path a number; nature takes the path with the smallest one. x′ means how fast x changes. The dots on a trail are the ticks of a clock.

The slide. First you draw the slide you think is fastest from A to B. The formula gives your slide a number, then shows the real fastest slide and its number.

The changes. Nine cards. Each shows a trip and a change: start the clock later, move the picture, turn it, or re-tick the clock fast here and slow there. You say: it matters, or it doesn't. Then you see the new trip in blue next to a dashed line showing where it would go if the change didn't matter, and the formula's number before and after.

The prizes. A change that doesn't matter wins a prize: a number you can work out at every moment of the trip that stays the same all along it. The graph under the picture draws that number along the real trip (flat) and along a fake one (wobbly). Prizes go on the shelf. If you said "it matters" when it didn't, the prize still goes on the shelf, marked yellow.

The empty prize. One prize stays flat on every path. You can draw paths and try to make its line move. You can't.

The end. Your shelf, one sentence, and what the paper says.
