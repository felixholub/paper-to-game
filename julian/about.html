Julian's Window is a game built from Julian of Norwich, Revelations of Divine Love, in Grace Warrack's translation (1901), chapters i–xxxii: the sixteen shewings of 8 May 1373 as she wrote them up twenty years later. Online at ccel.org. The text is the authority where the two disagree; chapters xxxiii–lxxxvi were not used.

What the game is built on

In the Seventh Shewing (ch. xv) Julian is filled with sureness, then "turned and left to myself in heaviness", then comforted, "now that one, and now that other, divers times — I suppose about twenty times". She checks the obvious cause and rules it out: "in this time I sinned not wherefore I should be left to myself"; "also I deserved not to have this blessed feeling." She concludes that the swing is given freely, that "He keepeth us even alike secure in woe and in weal", and that we should not "follow the feelings of pain in sorrow and mourning for them, but ... suddenly pass over." The game is that night: the heaviness comes and goes on a schedule drawn before your first look, it follows nothing you do, and the blanket is the same in every panel.

Where she looks is her one act all night, and the sights are the text's, in its order: the garland bleeding (ch. iv), the drops like pellets, herring scales and rain off the eaves (vii), the face half covered and "seeking is as good as beholding" (x), the scourging (xii), the laugh (xiii), the drying and "I thirst" (xvi–xvii), "Look up to Heaven" and "Nay; I may not" (xix), the change of cheer with "no time between" (xxi), "Art thou well pleased?" (xxii), the side (xxiv), His mother (xxv), "It is I" (xxvi). Within: the hazelnut (v), "sin is no deed" (xi), "Is any pain like this?" (xvii), the check of the cause (xv), "It behoved that there should be sin; but all shall be well" (xxvii), "I have made well the most harm" (xxix), and the hidden part (xxx, xxxii).

Mechanism: a toy

The game runs a model built to illustrate the text's argument, not a model the text contains. It demonstrates that a night can be played in which the feeling swings on its own clock, follows nothing the player does, and leaves the keeping untouched; and that the sights can be reached in the text's order by looking. It assumes the toy: ten beats, one per sight at the cross, so that no night shows everything; the twenty swings of one shewing compressed to about five or six across the night, on a seeded random schedule with the first heaviness forced by the third beat; a window that carries "Look up to Heaven" and the hour; the horror "away from the Cross" (ch. iii) drawn as scribbles at the edges; a sight dreadful in heaviness and sweet in sureness where ch. vii says one sight was both at once; the change of cheer tied to being at the cross when the weather turns, and only past the drying (ch. xxi); the morning's "you did not die" from ch. iii and viii, moved to the end of the night; the blanket for "He is our clothing" (ch. v); the questions asked when the player chooses rather than when He shows. The text establishes an argument from a vision, not a finding: that the swing was not caused by her sin, that the keeping is constant, that the right act is to pass over. Under this game's assumptions those three hold by construction; the game is a way of meeting them, not evidence for them.

The taking. On about one night in three the sickness takes you before morning, at the end of one of the last four beats, drawn from the night's number before your first look, like the weather, and never from where you looked. That is a counterfactual: she asked for a sickness "so hard as to death" (ch. ii) and was not taken; the pain left her all at once (ch. iii) and she read her survival as a sign the shewing was "for them that should live" (ch. viii). The taking is drawn from what she was shown of it, not from what happened to her: the Fifteenth Shewing (ch. i: "we shall suddenly be taken from all our pain and from all our woe, and of His Goodness we shall come up above") and ch. xxi ("betwixt that one and that other shall be no time"). It is not a losing: it is the ending she wanted, and the same shape as the weather, which is the lesson once more.

Every number and every line the game says sits in a config block at the top of mechanism.js, tagged with its chapter or marked as our choice. The weather is drawn from the night's number, shown on the morning page, so a night can be replayed.
