          How to play

          Your aimUranium makes the air carry electricity. Find what else does.
          1. The bench. Whatever lies on the lower brass plate, if it sends out rays, lets the air between the two plates carry a tiny electric current. The current pushes the spot of light along the scale, to the right.
          2. Your thumb. The slider lets a weight down onto a quartz crystal. Squeezed by the weight, the quartz sends back the opposite electricity and pushes the spot to the left. The faster you let the weight down, the harder it pushes.
          3. Write it down. Press WRITE IT DOWN whenever you like. Your page keeps the push you had at that moment, as a plain number, and a big balloon rises from the bench: what your page now believes, right or wrong.
          4. Your page. Each number is drawn as a panel. The yellow bar is what the thing's uranium alone could give, the blue bar the number you wrote, the dashed line your own pure uranium. A few lines after the blue bar mean the spot was still running when you wrote it down. You have nine panels; the first is pure uranium.
          5. The shelf. Tap a jar or a rock to read its label: what it is, who sent it, how much uranium it holds. TEST IT puts it on the plate and spends a panel. You can test anything again; your page believes the latest number.
          6. The last pages. After nine panels your ideas are put together into a physics, three laws, and drawn as a world: what life would look like if your note were true. Then ours, and where the two part.

          About

          The game in one line

          Push back on the electrometer's spot of light with Marie Curie's quartz, test what is on her shelf, and write down what you measure. Every number plants an idea; at the end your ideas are put together into a physics and drawn as a world, what life would be like if your note were true, beside ours.

          What the paper reports

          Marie Skłodowska Curie measured how strongly different substances make the air conduct electricity. One plate of a condenser (plates 8 cm across, 3 cm apart, 100 volts between them) was covered with a layer of the substance, and the tiny current through the air was measured with an electrometer and a piezoelectric quartz. She tested a great number of metals, salts, oxides and minerals. Her table gives the current for each, in units of 10−12 ampere: metallic uranium 24; black uranium oxide 27, green 18; the uranates about 12; hydrated uranic acid 6; uranyl nitrate and the uranium sulfates about 7; chalcolite made in the laboratory 9; thorium oxide 22 in a layer a quarter of a millimetre thick and 53 in a layer 6 mm thick; thorium sulfate 8; a tantalum salt 2; a niobium salt with cerium oxide under 1; pitchblende 83 (Johanngeorgenstadt), 67 (Joachimsthal and Příbram) and 16 (Cornwall); natural chalcolite 52; autunite 27; thorites 2 to 14; orangite 20; samarskite 11; five rare minerals 3 to 7; cleveite “very active”. Everything she studied and left out of the table is at least a hundred times less active than uranium.

          From the table: all the uranium compounds are active, and in general the more so the more uranium they contain. Thorium compounds are very active; thorium oxide beats metallic uranium. Uranium and thorium are the two elements of highest atomic weight. Cerium, niobium and tantalum seem slightly active. White phosphorus is very active, but probably for another reason: red phosphorus and the phosphates are not. The active minerals all contain active elements, and two uranium minerals, pitchblende and chalcolite, are much more active than uranium itself: “this fact is very remarkable, and leads one to believe that these minerals may contain an element much more active than uranium.” Chalcolite made by Debray's method from pure products is no more active than any other uranium salt.

          Then: a thicker layer gives more, a little for uranium compounds and a great deal for thorium oxide; the rays pass through thin metal, glass, ebonite and paper, and an aluminium leaf a hundredth of a millimetre thick lets through a fifth of them for the uranium compounds, a third for pitchblende and natural chalcolite, two-fifths for thorium, and seven-tenths for a thick layer of thorium oxide; good photographic impressions; a likeness to the secondary rays that Röntgen rays raise in metals (Sagnac). And a closing guess: perhaps all of space is crossed by rays like Röntgen's but far more penetrating, which only heavy elements such as uranium and thorium can absorb.

          Mechanism: reconstructed, with a fiction on top

          This game demonstrates, in a reconstruction built from the paper's table, that each thing on the shelf makes the air carry the current the table gives it, so that the push which holds the spot still is the table's number; and that, measured well, her readings lead to her conclusions: every uranium compound does it about in proportion to its uranium; thorium does it with no uranium; white phosphorus does, but red phosphorus and phosphate do not, so it is not the phosphorus itself; pitchblende and the chalcolite from a mine do far more than their uranium can, while the same chalcolite made from pure chemicals does not; so these minerals “may contain an element much more active than uranium”.

          What you write down (ours). A reading is the push on the slider at the moment you press WRITE IT DOWN, taken at face value. It is read against your own pure uranium (the latest one on your page) and the uranium on the jar's label. A few lines after a bar mean the spot was still running when you wrote it.

          The ideas and the worlds are fiction (ours). After each reading, the bench thinks what your page now believes, right or wrong. At the end your page settles four questions: where the energy comes from (inside the atom, a slow chemical fire, a wind from space caught by heavy atoms, the order of a crystal, or, if your page has the atom spending energy while old rocks hold nothing but their uranium, from nowhere at all); which matter does it; what hides in the rocks; and whether white phosphorus is chemistry or the atom. Only questions your page answered change nature; a jar you never measured leaves its question as in our world. The world of your note is drawn as if it were true and never corrected. These worlds are inventions, labelled as such; the facing page, ours, is history, and its dates are real. Nothing in them is a claim of the paper. One small irony is true: her own “slightly active” cerium, niobium and tantalum were most likely traces of thorium or uranium in the samples, and a page that copies her faithfully inherits it.

          It assumes how much uranium is in each thing (ours: from the chemical formulas for the compounds, a typical two-thirds for pitchblende; the paper gives none); a number for white phosphorus (ours: about a third more than uranium; the paper says only “very active”); the electrometer's behaviour (a spot that drifts with the difference between the plate's current and the quartz's, leaks back toward zero, and trembles; tuned for a thumb); a steady push in place of Curie's timed release of a weight (her thesis of 1903, not this note, describes how the quartz was worked: a weight let down by hand onto the quartz so that the electrometer stays at zero, the time read on a stopwatch); and the shelf: fifteen things from her dozens, sorted into drawers by the donors her footnote thanks. Every number sits in the config block at the top of the source, with the table row it came from; the table was transcribed by hand from a scan of typeset text.

          The paper establishes the currents it reports, under its conditions, and the argument it draws from them; its last step, that these minerals may contain an element much more active than uranium, is a conjecture in the note. It was borne out within the year (polonium and radium). Everything said about later history is on the page called Ours, with dates.

          What is cut

          From the shelf: black uranium oxide (27, a little above the metal; her “in general” covers it), hydrated uranic acid (6, well under its share), the uranates, the sulfates, thorium sulfate, the niobium salt with cerium oxide, the Cornish pitchblende (16, whose uranium content we cannot state), autunite, the thorites, orangite, samarskite, the five rare minerals, and cleveite. All are in the config block with their values. Not played: the thickness of the layer (except thorium oxide, thin and thick), the absorption by aluminium, the photographs, the Röntgen analogy and the closing guess, all above. The quartz's arithmetic (charge in proportion to the weight; current = charge ÷ time) is underneath; the player feels it as a push.

          The paper

          M. Skłodowska Curie, “Rayons émis par les composés de l'uranium et du thorium” (Rays emitted by the compounds of uranium and thorium), note presented by M. Lippmann, Comptes rendus hebdomadaires des séances de l'Académie des sciences 126 (1898): 1101–1103, session of 12 April 1898. No DOI. The Comptes rendus are on Gallica (Bibliothèque nationale de France); an English translation is in A. Romer (ed.), The Discovery of Radioactivity and Transmutation (Dover, 1964). The work was done at the École municipale de physique et de chimie industrielles in Paris; the uranium came from M. Moissan, the salts and oxides from M. Étard's laboratory, the minerals of known origin from M. Lacroix at the Muséum, rare pure oxides from M. Demarçay (her footnotes).
