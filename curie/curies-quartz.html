How to play

Your aimUranium makes the air carry electricity. Find what else does.
  1. The bench. Whatever lies on the lower brass plate, if it sends out rays, lets the air between the two plates carry a tiny electric current. The current pushes the spot of light along the scale, to the right.
  2. Your thumb. The slider lets a weight down onto a quartz crystal. Squeezed by the weight, the quartz pushes the spot back to the left. The faster you let the weight down, the harder it pushes.
  3. Write it down. When you think you know how strong the plate is, press WRITE IT DOWN. Your page keeps how hard you were pushing and what the spot was doing at that moment, and says how sure that makes the reading. Her own reading is shown next to yours.
  4. Your page. Each reading is drawn as a panel. The yellow bar is what the thing's uranium alone could give; the blue bar is your reading, with a line for how sure it is (an arrow if it is only "at least" or "at most"); the dashed line is pure uranium; the small black triangle is her reading. You have nine panels. The first is pure uranium.
  5. The shelf. Tap a jar or a rock to read its label: what it is, who sent it, how much uranium it holds. TEST IT puts it on the plate and spends a panel. You can test anything again, uranium too; each time is a new panel.
  6. The last page. After nine panels, your page is held up against her note: how many of its claims your own readings back.

About

The game in one line

Push back on the electrometer's spot of light with Marie Curie's quartz, test what is on her shelf, write down what you measure, and find what else, besides uranium, makes the air carry electricity: thorium, and something in pitchblende that is not uranium. At the end your page is held up against her note.

What the paper reports

Marie Skłodowska Curie measured how strongly different substances make the air conduct electricity. One plate of a condenser (plates 8 cm across, 3 cm apart, 100 volts between them) was covered with a layer of the substance, and the tiny current through the air was measured with an electrometer and a piezoelectric quartz. She tested a great number of metals, salts, oxides and minerals. Her table gives the current for each, in units of 10−12 ampere: metallic uranium 24; black uranium oxide 27, green 18; the uranates about 12; hydrated uranic acid 6; uranyl nitrate and the uranium sulfates about 7; chalcolite made in the laboratory 9; thorium oxide 22 in a layer a quarter of a millimetre thick and 53 in a layer 6 mm thick; thorium sulfate 8; a tantalum salt 2; a niobium salt with cerium oxide under 1; pitchblende 83 (Johanngeorgenstadt), 67 (Joachimsthal and Příbram) and 16 (Cornwall); natural chalcolite 52; autunite 27; thorites 2 to 14; orangite 20; samarskite 11; five rare minerals 3 to 7; cleveite “very active”. Everything she studied and left out of the table is at least a hundred times less active than uranium.

From the table: all the uranium compounds are active, and in general the more so the more uranium they contain. Thorium compounds are very active; thorium oxide beats metallic uranium. Uranium and thorium are the two elements of highest atomic weight. Cerium, niobium and tantalum seem slightly active. White phosphorus is very active, but probably for another reason: red phosphorus and the phosphates are not. The active minerals all contain active elements, and two uranium minerals, pitchblende and chalcolite, are much more active than uranium itself: “this fact is very remarkable, and leads one to believe that these minerals may contain an element much more active than uranium.” Chalcolite made by Debray's method from pure products is no more active than any other uranium salt.

Then: a thicker layer gives more, a little for uranium compounds and a great deal for thorium oxide; the rays pass through thin metal, glass, ebonite and paper, and an aluminium leaf a hundredth of a millimetre thick lets through a fifth of them for the uranium compounds, a third for pitchblende and natural chalcolite, two-fifths for thorium, and seven-tenths for a thick layer of thorium oxide; good photographic impressions; a likeness to the secondary rays that Röntgen rays raise in metals (Sagnac). And a closing guess: perhaps all of space is crossed by rays like Röntgen's but far more penetrating, which only heavy elements such as uranium and thorium can absorb.

Mechanism: reconstructed

This game demonstrates, in a reconstruction built from the paper's table, that each thing on the shelf makes the air carry the current the table gives it; that a reading taken with the spot standing still recovers the table's number, and one taken in a hurry gives only a bound; that, measured well, the uranium compounds read about in proportion to their uranium, or a little less; that thorium oxide and white phosphorus move the spot with no uranium in them, while red phosphorus and phosphate do not; and that pitchblende and the green chalcolite from a mine read far more than their uranium can give, while the same chalcolite made from pure chemicals does not.

How a reading is written down (ours). You may write it down at any moment. If the spot had been standing in the middle of the scale for a while, the page keeps a value: the charge balance over those seconds (the quartz's push, plus whatever is left on the electrometer), with an error bar that narrows the longer the spot stood. If the spot was still running right, the page keeps “at least” your push; running left, “at most”; not yet settled, “roughly”, give or take a lot. These are what the instrument honestly says at that moment; every reading of this kind contains her value. Labels on the panels come from your own readings, and the yardstick for what a thing's uranium could give is your own reading of pure uranium. Her reading is the black triangle.

The score (ours, from her text). Six claims of her note can be tested on this shelf: every uranium compound does it, about in proportion to its uranium; thorium does it with no uranium; thorium oxide beats pure uranium (her thick layer; her thin one does not quite); white phosphorus does it but red phosphorus and phosphates do not; pitchblende and chalcolite do far more than their uranium can; the same chalcolite made from pure chemicals does not. A claim counts when your own readings back it. A page of jars that read zero backs nothing, however exactly it is measured.

It assumes how much uranium is in each thing (ours: from the chemical formulas for the compounds, a typical two-thirds for pitchblende; the paper gives none, and her rocks read more than their uranium for any share from 40 to 88 in 100); a number for white phosphorus (ours: about a third more than uranium; the paper says only “very active”); the electrometer's behaviour (a spot that drifts with the difference between the plate's current and the quartz's, leaks back toward zero, and trembles; tuned for a thumb); a steady push in place of Curie's timed release of a weight (her thesis of 1903, not this note, describes how the quartz was worked: a weight let down by hand onto the quartz so that the electrometer stays at zero, the time read on a stopwatch; the quantity measured is the same); how sure a reading is, as described above; one reading per substance, as the table gives it; and the shelf: fifteen things from her dozens, sorted into drawers by the donors her footnote thanks (which donor sent the thorium oxide and the tantalum salt is ours). The tantalum salt (her “slightly active”) is too faint to see without holding the spot still for several seconds. Every number sits in the config block at the top of the source, with the table row it came from; the table was transcribed by hand from a scan of typeset text.

The paper establishes the currents it reports, under its conditions, and the argument it draws from them: activity belongs to uranium and thorium wherever they are; two uranium minerals are much more active than uranium; the same mineral made from pure products is not; so these minerals may contain an element much more active than uranium. In the note that last is a conjecture, not a result. It was borne out within the year (polonium and radium), which the last page says, marked as after this note.

What is cut

From the shelf: black uranium oxide (27, a little above the metal; her “in general” covers it), hydrated uranic acid (6, well under its share), the uranates, the sulfates, thorium sulfate, the niobium salt with cerium oxide, the Cornish pitchblende (16, whose uranium content we cannot state), autunite, the thorites, orangite, samarskite, the five rare minerals, and cleveite. All are in the config block with their values. Not played: the thickness of the layer (except thorium oxide, thin and thick), the absorption by aluminium, the photographs, the Röntgen analogy and the closing guess, all above. The quartz's arithmetic (charge in proportion to the weight; current = charge ÷ time) is underneath; the player feels it as a push.

The paper

M. Skłodowska Curie, “Rayons émis par les composés de l'uranium et du thorium” (Rays emitted by the compounds of uranium and thorium), note presented by M. Lippmann, Comptes rendus hebdomadaires des séances de l'Académie des sciences 126 (1898): 1101–1103, session of 12 April 1898. No DOI. The Comptes rendus are on Gallica (Bibliothèque nationale de France); an English translation is in A. Romer (ed.), The Discovery of Radioactivity and Transmutation (Dover, 1964). The work was done at the École municipale de physique et de chimie industrielles in Paris; the uranium came from M. Moissan, the salts and oxides from M. Étard's laboratory, the minerals of known origin from M. Lacroix at the Muséum, rare pure oxides from M. Demarçay (her footnotes).
