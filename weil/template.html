The Iliad, or the Poem of Force

Force turns whoever it touches into a thing.

You are the soul inside a warrior. Each day of a war, a man comes before you. While Zeus's scales hold your pan up, you decide what to do with him; when they turn, a man stands over you.

How to play

Force is yours, for now. Keep your soul through the war.

What you see

You are the soul inside the warrior on the left of the vase. Your figure is clay while it has a soul: six fine lines, at your knees, thighs, belly, chest, mouth and eye. When you lose one, stone climbs from your feet: black glaze, with only its outline scratched in. A figure that is stone all through is a thing.

Above the figures hang Zeus's golden scales. While your pan (the left one) is up, force is yours that day: a man comes before you and you decide what to do with him. When your pan is down, force is his: a man stands over you. The scales turn by themselves, day by day; what you do with force changes how far they swing.

What you can do

  • Strike: kill him (or beat him down).
  • Take: take him, or her, alive, as a slave or a prize.
  • Pass: go on as if he were not there.
  • Raise: give him your hand.

A sherd shown cracked cannot be played on this man; it says why. Touch a sherd to see the gesture on your figure, then the long sherd underneath to do it.

When your pan is down: Plead (clasp his knees), Obey (be his, and live) or Stand (face him). A man who is still clay can hear a plea; a man of stone cannot.

What to watch for

An armed man who is nearly all stone will strike if you do not. When your pan stands very high, or when little of your soul is left, your arm starts to move toward him by itself: then a single touch on a sherd acts at once, and if you touch nothing, the spear goes in. At the very top, or with no soul left, there is no interval at all.

Twelve days, the last a night. A life ends on the night, or earlier if you die or become a man's slave. The vase is then turned round, and you see every day of it.
