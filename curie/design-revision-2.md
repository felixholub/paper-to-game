# Curie's Quartz — revision 2 design: the worlds of wrong notes

*Agreed with the author on 1 Nov 2026 (no hint; balloons from the bench, no Curie drawn; ideas planted per experiment, assembled only at the end). Built as revision 2; where the build differs, the memo says so.*

## 0. What is decided

- **The player writes numbers.** A reading is the push on the slider at the moment of WRITE IT DOWN, taken at face value. A careless reading is a wrong number, and a wrong number leads somewhere.
- **We draw the conclusions.** After every panel, a balloon in the comic says what this page now believes, right or wrong, in the voice of the bench. The player never picks a conclusion; they pick jars and numbers, and the page thinks for them.
- **No score.** The last pages put two physics side by side, and two worlds: the one your note describes, and ours.
- **"If it were true."** Each wrong conclusion becomes a law of nature in that world, never discovered to be wrong. The world is drawn as it would be: weird, consistent, and labelled as fiction.
- **Electricity comes back.** Readings are said as electricity: "the air carried about half as much electricity as with pure uranium." The slider stays a push. The bench says plainly that the push is the quartz sending back the opposite electricity.
- **Default, reversible:** a panel whose spot was still running when it was written down carries a few speed lines. Nothing says what they mean; the attentive player will see it.

## 1. The loop

Unchanged: the bench, the shelf, nine panels, any jar again for a panel. Changed: after WRITE IT DOWN the game first says, in its own voice, what the air carried ("the air carried about half as much electricity as with your pure uranium. Hers: none"), and then **a thought balloon** rises from the bench. Its tail leaves the panel at the bottom edge, toward the unseen person at the bench: the player. No figure of Curie is drawn. Every experiment plants an idea, good or bad, and the page does not judge it; a second balloon appears when a reading clashes with an earlier one. A retest replaces the earlier reading of that jar ("Second look: …"); the latest reading of each jar is what the page believes. Under the album page on the shelf, the ideas so far are kept as small balloons.

Nothing is assembled until the ninth panel. Then three comic pages and a facing one:
1. **Your ideas:** every balloon planted, pinned up with its panel number; clashing ones drawn bumping.
2. **Your physics:** the ideas fused into a named physics, a title card with three laws, each with call-outs to the panels that made it.
3. **Your world:** one big splash panel, a ligne claire panorama of life under those laws. The landmark comes from where the energy comes from; three framed vignettes beneath it come from your other ideas (or, where you planted none, from that world's own life); numbered call-outs tie each oddity to its panel; a paragraph says how life goes there. Titled **THE WORLD OF YOUR NOTE (IF IT WERE TRUE)**.
4. **Ours:** the same composition drawn true, with a few dated captions, and **where they part**, in words. Then the worlds this browser has not seen yet, as greyed titles, and START A NEW ALBUM. No hint.

## 2. From a number to what it shows

Let *x* be the number written down, *U* the latest pure-uranium reading on the page, *u* the uranium share on the jar's label.

- **Things that hold uranium** (the salts, the rocks, the copy): the ratio *q = x / (u·U)*, what it gave against what its uranium alone could give.
  - NONE: *x* below a twentieth of *U*
  - LOW: *q* under a half
  - ABOUT: *q* from a half to 1.3 (her table puts every salt between 0.62 and 0.88)
  - HIGH: *q* above 1.3 (her rocks are 4 to 6)
- **Things without uranium:** against *U* directly.
  - NONE: under a twentieth
  - FAINT: a twentieth to a sixth
  - ACTIVE: a sixth to 1.3
  - STRONG: above 1.3
- **Pure uranium itself** has no band. It is the yardstick, and a wrong yardstick is the most powerful wrong number on the page: read low, every salt becomes HIGH; read high, every salt becomes LOW.

## 3. The balloons

Every line below is a draft. "→" names what the page now believes (section 4).

**Pure uranium** (any reading): *"So this is pure uranium: the air between the plates carries this much electricity. Everything else gets measured against it."* On a retest: *"Second look at pure uranium. Now everything is measured against this."*

**Green uranium oxide, uranyl nitrate** (the salts):
- ABOUT: *"About what its uranium gives. It's the uranium that does it, whatever it's joined to."* → the atom.
- HIGH: *"Stronger than its uranium could be! Joined to oxygen, uranium burns brighter… is this a kind of fire?"* → chemistry.
- LOW: *"Far weaker than its uranium. Does joining uranium to other atoms smother it?"* → chemistry.
- NONE: *"Nothing?! A uranium salt that doesn't do it? Then it isn't the uranium at all…"* → chemistry, strongly.

**Thin and thick thorium oxide:**
- NONE: *"Thorium: nothing. Uranium is alone, then. A freak metal?"* → uranium alone.
- FAINT: *"A whisper from thorium. Something, but hardly."*
- ACTIVE: *"Thorium does it, with no uranium in it! Not one strange metal: the two heaviest atoms there are. Hmm… does the table of elements end where atoms start to leak?"* → the heaviest atoms.
- STRONG: *"Thorium beats pure uranium! And a thicker layer gives more: thorium lets its own rays through."* → the heaviest atoms.

**Lead, gold:**
- NONE: *"Lead is heavy, and nothing. So it isn't weight alone."*
- FAINT: *"A faint leak near lead? Perhaps every heavy atom does it, a little."*
- ACTIVE or STRONG: *"Wow! The air carries electricity near lead! Every heavy atom does it, the heavier the more… as if they caught something raining through space."* → the sky wind. (This is her own closing guess, on p. 1103, turned into a law by one wrong number.)
- Gold adds: *"Gold too! Every bank vault must be crackling."*

**Copper:**
- NONE: *"Copper: nothing. Good: it isn't just any metal."*
- ACTIVE or more: *"Copper too?! Then every metal leaks electricity. The telegraph wires must be humming."* → everything.

**The tantalum salt:**
- NONE: *"Tantalum: nothing I can see."*
- FAINT: *"A faint leak from tantalum. The rare metals, a little."* (her own conclusion, p. 1102)
- ACTIVE or more: *"Tantalum, nearly as strong as uranium! The rare metals all do it?"*

**White phosphorus:**
- NONE: *"White phosphorus: nothing."*
- ACTIVE or more: *"White phosphorus does it! And phosphorus is in every bone… is the stuff of life electric?"* → living light, until red phosphorus or a phosphate says otherwise.

**Red phosphorus, calcium phosphate** (after white phosphorus moved the spot):
- NONE: *"Red phosphorus: nothing. Same element, another form: so it was never the phosphorus. The white kind was just burning slowly in the air."* → chemistry, for phosphorus only (hers).
- ACTIVE: *"Red phosphorus too! It is the phosphorus atom, a light one. So weight has nothing to do with it."* → living light, confirmed.

**Pitchblende (Saxony, Bohemia):**
- NONE or LOW: *"Less than its uranium. That rock is uranium and dirt, nothing more."* → no hidden element.
- ABOUT: *"Just about its uranium. Nothing else in that rock."* → no hidden element.
- HIGH: *"More than its uranium can give?! Something else is in that rock, something far stronger than uranium!"* → a hidden element. In a sky-wind page: *"…something heavier than uranium, hiding in the rock?"* In a chemistry page: *"…a fiercer fuel?"*

**Chalcolite from a mine:**
- HIGH: *"More than its uranium! Something in the rock… or the way the crystal is built?"* (left open until the copy)
- ABOUT or below: as pitchblende.

**Chalcolite made by you** (if the mine's chalcolite is HIGH):
- ABOUT or below: *"My own chalcolite is ordinary. So it isn't the crystal: the mine's crystal holds something mine doesn't!"* → the hidden element, confirmed (her argument).
- HIGH: *"Mine is as strong as the mine's! It's the crystal itself that does it, not anything hidden."* → the crystal lens.

**Clashes**, the second balloon:
- Two pitchblendes in different bands: *"Wait… two mines, two answers?"*
- Lead and gold disagree: *"Gold is heavy too, and they don't agree. Is it weight or isn't it?"*
- A salt ABOUT and another HIGH: *"Wait. One salt follows its uranium and the other doesn't. Which is it, the atom or the chemistry?"*
- Thorium NONE and lead ACTIVE: *"Lead does it but thorium doesn't? Thorium is heavier than lead…"*

## 4. From the page to a physics

Four questions. The latest reading of each jar decides; a jar never measured leaves that question as in our world. Only the conclusions you drew change nature.

**I. Where does the energy come from?** Settled in this order of precedence:
1. **The sky wind**, if lead or gold reads ACTIVE or STRONG.
2. **The slow fire** (chemistry), if any salt reads NONE, LOW or HIGH, or the yardstick makes them so.
3. **The crystal lens**, if the copy reads HIGH.
4. **The atom**, otherwise. Then the rocks split it:
   - rocks HIGH: **the restless atom**, which is hers and ours;
   - rocks ABOUT or below: **the bottomless atom** (see the deep twist below);
   - rocks never measured: ours, with the hidden element found later by someone else.

A lower-precedence finding is not lost. It enters the world as a secondary law ("…and chemistry tunes how much a thing catches").

**II. Which matter does it?**
- **Uranium alone:** thorium NONE, nothing else active.
- **The heaviest atoms:** thorium active, nothing light (hers).
- **By weight:** lead or gold active.
- **Living light:** the phosphorus atom (white phosphorus active with no control, or a control that also reads active).
- **Everything:** copper active, or both "by weight" and "living light".
- The rare metals (tantalum FAINT or more) add a line.

**III. Is something hidden in the rocks?**
- **Yes:** a rock HIGH, and the copy, if made, not HIGH.
- **No:** rocks measured and ABOUT or below.
- **The crystal:** the copy HIGH.
- **Never looked:** ours, found later by someone else.

**IV. White phosphorus:** chemistry (hers), or the phosphorus atom (living light).

**The deep twist: the bottomless atom.** If the page says the energy comes from inside the uranium atom, and also that old rocks hold nothing but their uranium, both cannot be true in our physics. An atom that spends energy must turn into something, and in a rock a billion years old the somethings pile up. That is exactly why pitchblende is stronger than its uranium. A world where both are true is a world where **energy comes from nowhere**: the atom spends and never changes. This is historically grounded. In 1898–99 the source of the energy was the great puzzle, and Curie herself weighed whether it broke the principle of Carnot.¹ The game says this at the end, in plain words, as the deepest lesson the paper holds.

## 5. The worlds

Each world has a name, three laws, a landmark, three vignettes of its own, and a paragraph. The dates below feed the paragraphs and, for ours, the captions; the panorama is one picture, not a strip. Oddities from the modifiers take the vignette slots first. Every world ends with the same small caption: *"In our world, someone would have caught it within a year."*

### Hers, and ours: **The restless atom**
*Laws:* the energy comes from inside the heaviest atoms; atoms spend it and turn into other elements; old rocks are full of what they turned into.
- **1898:** from tons of pitchblende, two new elements: polonium (July) and radium (December). *Drawing: the shed, a cauldron, a glowing dish.*
- **1903:** radium keeps itself warmer than the room, for ever, it seems; Becquerel and the two Curies share the Nobel Prize. *Drawing: a thermometer beside a dish of radium.*
- **1912:** Rutherford fires radium's rays at gold leaf (1909–11): the atom has a tiny, heavy heart. That same year, Hess's balloon finds rays from space (§ the sky wind). *Drawing: gold foil, rays bouncing back.*
- **1945:** atoms of uranium are split (1938); a million times the energy of any fire, first as a weapon, then in power stations. *Drawing, with restraint: a power station's dome; no cloud.*
- **Today:** rocks are dated by the atoms that changed in them: the Earth is 4.5 billion years old. Rays treat cancer. The table of elements ends where atoms fall apart, at 118, the last ones made by hand. *Drawing: a rock cut open, with a clock in it.*

### **The slow fire** (a salt off its uranium, or the yardstick off)
*Laws:* uranium's rays are a slow chemical fire, like white phosphorus glowing in air; how uranium is joined to other atoms sets how bright it burns; it uses itself up.
- **1898:** uranium salts outshine the metal: the rays belong to the chemists. *Drawing: flasks glowing brighter than an ingot.*
- **1903:** seal uranium in a jar and it goes dark; warm it and it brightens; every year the old samples are dimmer. *Drawing: a jar going dark, a thermometer.*
- **1912:** atoms never change. No heart in the atom, no new elements: alchemy stays a fairy tale for good. *Drawing: a solid ball of an atom on a pedestal, labelled FINAL.*
- **1945:** no atomic energy; the century runs on coal and oil alone. *Drawing: chimneys.*
- **Today:** nothing keeps the Earth warm from inside, so Lord Kelvin's young Earth of 20 to 40 million years stands, and nothing lets the Sun burn longer either. Darwin's theory spends the century short of time. *Drawing: Kelvin with a thermometer in a cooling globe; Darwin with an hourglass.*

*Where it parts from ours:* real radioactivity ignores temperature and sealing, and does not fade with chemistry; the Earth is 4.5 billion years old.

### **The sky wind** (lead or gold read as active)
*Laws:* space is crossed by an invisible wind; heavy atoms catch it, the heavier the more, and give it out as rays; nothing is used up, the energy blows in.
- **1898:** lead and gold make the air carry electricity: heavy metals are sails. *Drawing: gold coins and a lead pipe, sparking.*
- **1903:** down a deep mine, uranium falls silent; on a mountain it roars. The century's question: where does the wind blow from? *Drawing: miners with an electrometer, the spot asleep.*
- **1912:** Victor Hess's balloon finds the air conducts more the higher it rises: the wind, found! *Drawing: the balloon.* In ours too, which is the twist: rays from space are real (Hess, 1912, with this very instrument), but faint, and they do not power uranium.
- **1945:** sails of lead on the Alps catch the wind and feed the cities. *Drawing: lead sails on peaks, power lines running down.*
- **Today:** nurseries are built of light metals; bank vaults crackle; people go down mines for quiet. *Drawing: a child's room of aluminium.*

*With a hidden element:* "something heavier than uranium hides in the rocks": the hunt for element 93 in pitchblende. In ours, element 93 was made by hand in 1940.

### **The crystal lens** (the copy as strong as the mine's)
*Laws:* the order of a crystal multiplies uranium's rays; a well-grown crystal shines far brighter than the same atoms in a heap; order, not ingredients.
- **1898:** my own chalcolite is as strong as the mine's: grow the right crystal and uranium shines. *Drawing: a green crystal growing in a flask.*
- **1903:** crystal-growers race for the brightest lattice; grind a crystal and it dies. *Drawing: a crystal fair, prize ribbons.*
- **1912:** nobody hunts for new elements in pitchblende; there is no radium, and no heart found in the atom. *Drawing: a sack of pitchblende, unopened.*
- **1945:** a uranium crystal the size of a house lights a city. *Drawing: the crystal tower.*
- **Today:** crystal lamps everywhere; the inside of the atom never opened. *In ours, crystals that make light stronger came in 1960: the ruby laser. Light, not uranium.*

### **The bottomless atom** (energy from the atom, and rocks no more than their uranium)
*Laws:* the energy comes from inside the uranium atom; the atom never changes and never runs down; energy comes from nowhere.
- **1898:** old rocks hold nothing but uranium: the atom spends and stays the same. *Drawing: a rock, unchanged, beside an hourglass.*
- **1903:** a lump of uranium turns a wheel for ever. The patent office, which refused every perpetual-motion machine, accepts one. *Drawing: the uranium engine and a stamped patent.*
- **1912:** physics rewrites its first law: energy can come from nowhere. *Drawing: a blackboard, "energy is conserved" crossed out.*
- **1945:** uranium is the most precious metal on Earth; the mines of Joachimsthal are its capital. *Drawing: a town of uranium.*
- **Today:** free heat from every uranium stone; the Earth is warmed from within for ever, and as old as anyone likes. *Drawing: a town heated by a stone.*

*Where it parts from ours:* the paragraph of section 4, said simply. This is the world the paper's own argument rules out.

### Modifiers (an inset and a line in the panel they touch)
- **Uranium alone:** uranium is a freak: one strange metal, no pattern, no law, and the table of elements keeps its secret. *Inset: uranium on a museum pedestal.*
- **Living light:** bones make the air carry electricity; doctors listen to skeletons with electrometers; graveyards crackle. *Inset: a doctor with an electrometer to a patient's ribs.*
- **Everything:** every metal leaks; wires cannot hold a charge; and the air, a little conductive everywhere, cannot build up the charge for lightning: storms without thunderbolts. *Inset: a storm with no lightning, people puzzled under umbrellas.*
- **The rare metals:** cerium, niobium and tantalum a little. In ours, this is her own small mistake: those metals are not measurably radioactive, and her readings were most likely traces of thorium or uranium in the samples. It is the same reasoning about impurities that found radium.

## 6. Where they part (the closing text)

In words, no score. Three short paragraphs:
1. **Your physics against hers.** Each of your laws, and whether it is hers.
2. **The numbers that did it.** For each wrong law, the panel and the number, for example: *"Panel 2: you wrote pure uranium at about half her reading. That one number made every salt look stronger than the metal, and gave your world its slow fire."*
3. **Your world against ours.** The strip that differs most, in one sentence.

When the page is hers, the closing text says so: *"Your page is her note. Your world is the one you live in."* Then the replay line: *"Write different numbers, and see another world."*

## 7. Honesty

- Every alternative world is fiction and labelled so on the page, at the top of the strip: **THE WORLD OF YOUR NOTE (IF IT WERE TRUE)**.
- The facing strip is real, and every date is checked before the build: polonium and radium (1898); Rutherford and Soddy (1902); radium's heat (Curie and Laborde, 1903); Nobel (1903); Boltwood's dating (1907); Geiger, Marsden and Rutherford (1909–11); Hess (1912); Holmes on the Earth's age (1913); fission (1938–39); 1945; element 93 (1940); the ruby laser (1960); Patterson's 4.55 billion years (1956); element 118. ¹ Curie weighing Carnot's principle (1899, *Revue générale des sciences*) is to be checked before it is used.
- The physics of each world has to follow from its laws. The checks below make sure no strip contradicts its own laws.
- 1945 is drawn with restraint in every world.

No real person is drawn: Kelvin's young Earth is a thermometer in a globe, Darwin's shortage of time an hourglass.
