How to play

The machine

Lovelace's machine never thinks. It reads your chain of cards from the top, one card at a time, and does what each card says. When it runs out of cards, it stops.

Hanging cards

Tap a card at the bottom to hang it at the end of your chain. Tap × to take a card off. Only so many cards fit. Then tap Run the loom and watch. The dashed squares are the order; each stitch the loom weaves fills one. A wrong stitch gets a black frame.

Go back

The machine counts with wheels: numbers inside it. Before you run the loom, put a number on each wheel with − and +.

A Go back card takes 1 off its wheel and looks at the result. If the wheel isn't 0 yet, the machine goes back up the chain to the card you chose on the Go back card (“card 1”, “card 2” …); the arc on the left shows the jump. If the wheel shows 0, the machine carries on down the chain.

Set cards

A wheel that reaches 0 stays at 0. A Set card puts a new number on a wheel. That is what a loop inside a loop needs: the inner wheel must be set again before every round of the outer loop.

Fewest cards

When the bell rings you are told the fewest cards that can do the job. On the hard looms there is no spare room at all.

Stuck?

After three runs that don't ring the bell, you can ask to see a way. It's one way, not the only one.

About the paper

The game in one line

A machine that does exactly what its cards say. Six looms are dealt at random from a library, two easy, two harder, two hard: you hang as few cards as you can to weave each order, with wheels that hold numbers, Go back cards that take one off and look, and Set cards that put a number back, so that on the hard looms a loop sits inside a loop. Then a switch makes the same cards play a tune and do sums, and you ask the machine to make something up. It can't.

What the paper does

In 1842 L. F. Menabrea published a sketch of Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, a calculating machine driven by punched cards. In 1843 Ada Lovelace translated it and added seven Notes, longer than the sketch itself. The Notes are the text this game is built from.

Note A says the Engine weaves algebraical patterns just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves, and that it works on operations, not on any one kind of thing: it could act on anything whose relations can be written in symbols, music included. Notes C and E explain backing: the cards can be sent back so that a few of them do duty again and again, a cycle. Note F shows three cards doing the work of three hundred and thirty. Note G gives a full chain of cards for the Numbers of Bernoulli, twenty-five cards that serve however great n may be, and warns that the Engine has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.

What is the paper's, and what is ours

The loom is Lovelace's own picture from Note A, made literal: the Engine did arithmetic, it never wove cloth. The counting is done as the Notes say an Engine counts: with a number in the Store and cards that operate on it. A wheel is such a number, put on by hand before the run, as Note B inscribes the data on the columns. The Go back card is her Operation 7 in Note G joined to her backing in Note C: a subtraction whose result the Engine feels; if it is not yet zero, the cards are backed to an earlier card, and if it is zero, the Engine goes on (Menabrea's counter, too, has a unit taken off each time until it marks zero). A Set card is a card of numbers (Memoir p. 15): nothing resets a wheel by itself, so a loop inside a loop, her cycle of cycles (Note E), needs a card that puts the inner number back. In her own tables the subtraction and the decision are separate steps; here they share one card. Counting cards is her yardstick too: Notes D, F and G all count how few cards will do. The puzzles, the fewest-card counts (found by trying every possible chain) and the switch's tune and sums are ours.

The coda is hers. It runs Lovelace's own table from Note G, operation by operation, on a working model of the Engine's card language, and prints the first four Numbers of Bernoulli: 1/6, −1/30, 1/42, −1/30, with nine, fourteen and twenty-five cards. Two small repairs: one division is done the way round her text requires (the printed table has it reversed), and a sign is flipped so the Numbers print with the sign her formula gives. Her cycle (operations 13 to 23, in brown) is simply repeated for the fourth Number.

The Engine was never built. Nothing here is evidence about the machine; it is the Notes' ideas, running.
