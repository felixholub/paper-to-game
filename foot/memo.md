This is the paper in which the runaway tram first appears.

## 2. What the paper hands over

**Primary: an argument.** A set of paired cases in which the numbers are held fixed (one life against five) and our verdicts differ, and two rival principles offered to explain the difference:

- **The doctrine of the double effect (DDE).** It is sometimes permissible to bring about by *oblique* intention (a foreseen side effect) what one may not *directly* intend (as an end or as a means). Its defenders use it to forbid the judge, the serum and the torture while allowing the tram, the scarce drug and the rescue (pp. 1–3).
- **Foot's alternative: negative and positive duties.** The duty not to injure (negative) is stricter than the duty to bring aid (positive). Negative against negative: do the least injury (the tram driver). Positive against positive: aid the greater number (the scarce drug). Negative against positive: one may not, in general, inflict injury to bring aid (the judge, the serum, the torture) (pp. 4–5). Deliberately letting someone die *in order that* he die is "inclined" to count as a violation of the negative duty (the beggar, p. 4).

The two principles agree on every classic pair. Foot then builds the case that splits them: **the gas** (pp. 4–5). Five patients can be saved by manufacturing a gas that inevitably releases lethal fumes into the room of a sixth who cannot be moved. His death is "of no use to us", "clearly a side effect", so DDE permits it; it is injury to bring aid, so Foot's principle forbids it; "yet it surely is different" from the scarce drug, and "the relatives of the gassed patient would presumably be successful if they sued".

A second divergence is in the abortion section (p. 5): the **cave** (the man stuck in the mouth of the cave, who will drown with the others if nothing is done). Anyone who holds that direct intention of an innocent death is never justifiable must forbid the dynamite; Foot: "there is no serious conflict of interests here", "it is reasonable that the action that will save someone should be done", and the absolute edict "must here conflict with that of most reasonable men".

**Secondary: a reply.** Anscombe (pp. 6–7 of the PDF, after Foot's text) disputes one point: that in the scarce-drug case one *must* save the five. Giving the whole dose to the one wrongs no one ("who can say he is wronged?"). Read on About, not played.

**Context: abortion.** Foot's purpose is to test the Catholic doctrine's verdicts on operations that kill a fetus (hysterectomy permitted, craniotomy forbidden). She finds parallels among adults for three medical cases and states her conclusions tentatively ("I have not been arguing for or against these points of view"). Read on About, not played.

## 3. The discovery, and its kind

**Identification.** Two candidate explanations of why the driver may steer but the judge may not frame are perfectly confounded in the familiar cases: wherever the death is *aimed at*, it is also *harm done to help others*; wherever it is a *side effect*, it is also harm traded against harm, or help traded against help. Foot designs the case that breaks the confound — a death that is a side effect *and* harm done to help others — and our verdict follows the second variable, not the first. Her conclusion: "the distinction between direct and oblique intention plays only a quite subsidiary role in determining what we say in these cases, while the distinction between avoiding injury and bringing aid is very important indeed" (p. 5), and "even if we reject the doctrine of the double effect we are not forced to the conclusion that the size of the evil must always be our guide" (p. 5).

A **counterintuitive** rider for a stranger who arrives knowing "the trolley problem": in the paper that invented it, steering the tram is the *easy* case — "we should say, without hesitation, that the driver should steer for the less occupied track" (p. 2). The puzzle is the judge.

## 4. The core narrative

A bad man wants to make good people do terrible things, and he has a trick: he puts them where every choice costs a life, and lets their own rules do the rest. If your rule is "always save the most", he can make you kill anyone, just by threatening more. If your rule is "never mean to kill an innocent person", he can't make you kill on purpose — but he can arrange for the killing to be a side effect, and then your rule waves it through, and everyone else is horrified. Only one rule stops him everywhere: never hurt one person to help others. Hurting one to stop hurting five is all right; choosing whom to help is all right; hurting someone so that you can help someone else is not.

## 5. Who lives in the paper

- **The agents who must choose:** the tram driver, the judge, the doctors with the scarce drug, the doctors who want a serum, the hospital with the gas, the potholers with the dynamite, the rescuer, the surgeon. Each controls one act (steer, frame, allocate, kill, manufacture, blast). Candidate player for a dilemma walk-through. Concept C uses the "we" behind them instead.
- **The bad man.** "Some tyrant should threaten to torture five men if we ourselves would not torture one … A mad murderer, known to keep his promises, could thus make it our duty to kill some innocent citizen to prevent him from killing two" (p. 3). The argument for DDE is that without it we are "hopelessly in the power of bad men". He controls the situation; he wants the good to do evil. Candidate agent: concept A.
- **The philosopher.** "Philosophers have arranged that floodwaters should be rising within the cave" (p. 1). She controls the case: holds the numbers at one against five and varies one feature at a time. Candidate experimenter: concept B. (The bad man and the philosopher do the same thing — build a situation that makes a rule give a terrible answer — for opposite reasons. Concept A uses that.)
- **The principles themselves:** the size of the evil ("must always be our guide"), DDE, negative/positive duties. Each is a machine that takes a case and returns a verdict. Candidate opponents.
- **"Most of us."** The paper's data are verdicts: "we should say, without hesitation", "most of us would be appalled", "yet it surely is different", "most reasonable men". Candidate subject (concept C), and in A and B the judge of outcomes.
- **The one, and the five.** The victim on the branch line; Salmond's "right not to be harmed" against the "right to be positively benefited" (p. 4). Anscombe's question is theirs: who is wronged? Considered as a player (dodging the tram: the "foothold" passage, p. 2); not a concept, because the foothold is the point Foot explicitly sets aside.

## 7. The aim and the verdict

**The aim** (start screen, top of the play screen, first line of How to play): *Disgrace three good people, using nothing but their own rules.*

"Disgrace" is defined where it first appears: *the audience turns on them* — they did, by their own rule, something most of us cannot forgive.

**The yardstick** is in the aim's units: three oval portraits across the top of the play screen. The moment a scheme disgraces its target, a red DISGRACED card is pasted across that portrait; when a target survives their three schemes, a silver CLEAN card. Under the current portrait, the schemes left for them (three amber pips).

**The verdict** (end screen) leads with three lines composed from what the player did:

1. *What you did:* e.g. "You disgraced Mr Most with the bargain and Doctor Double with the brew. Mrs Foot you could not touch."
2. *What the paper says:* "Philippa Foot, 1967: the doctrine Doctor Double lives by forbids aiming at an innocent death but lets one through as a side effect — and her gas case (your brew) shows that we find that just as wrong. What matters is not what you aim at but whether you harm one person to help others."
3. *What it means:* "A rule that only counts lives puts good people in a bad man's power. A rule about what you mean is half a shield. Foot's rule — never harm one to help others; if every way harms, harm the fewest; if you can only help some, help the most — gave you nothing."

Then the fate in words ("Two good names ruined; the third you could not touch"), then the map of holes. No number: the three portraits are the score.

## 8. Three concepts

**A. Foot's Bad Man** — *the reckless one.* *Position:* agent, the paper's tyrant ("putting us hopelessly in the power of bad men"). *Format:* an exploit hunt: three good people in turn, each with a rule (count lives; DDE; Foot's), three schemes against each, chosen from six that are the paper's own cases reset as traps (the tram with its brakes cut; the bargain; the medicine; the brew; the beggar; the cave). *Controls:* tap a scheme to preview the situation it creates; spring it. *Deliberating:* "What will his rule make him do here — and will the audience hate it?" Two predictions stacked: the rule, then the verdict. *Seen after:* a short silent film of the good person choosing by their rule, the result, and the stalls' reaction; the portrait stamped or not. *Discovery moment:* the Doctor refuses the bargain ("his death would be my means"); the player reads his rule again, sees that a death he only foresees is allowed, tries the tram (he steers; the audience cheers — not a win), then the brew: he brews, the man in the next room dies, and the stalls turn on him. *That's* what intention fails to catch. Then Mrs Foot refuses the brew. *Fun:* agency (the player's scheme, their outcome), anticipation (watching a rule run), discovery (the hole), mastery (by the third opponent the player reasons like Foot). *Replay:* the Doctor has a second hole (the cave: his rule makes him refuse, and seven drown for a scruple); the map of holes shows what was not tried.

**B. The Case Bench.** *Position:* experimenter (the philosopher). *Format:* minimal pairs. The judge's case stands on a bench with two lamps (DDE, Foot), both red. Three switches change one feature each — the death is *the means / a side effect*; you *harm / fail to help*; the five are endangered *by you / by someone else* — and the scene morphs into the paper's case with those features (or a blank plate: "no case in the paper"). *Aim:* find a case where the lamps disagree, then ask the stalls. *Deliberating:* which switch separates the theories? *Discovery:* flip "side effect" on the judge: the scene becomes the gas, DDE turns green, Foot stays red, the stalls side with Foot. *Fun:* discovery, some agency. *Replay:* find the second split (the cave).

**C. Two Mind-Readers.** *Position:* subject (the "we" whose verdicts the paper explains). *Format:* a deck of the paper's cases; before each, a DDE theorist and Foot each seal a prediction of the player's verdict; the player rules *allow / forbid*; the envelopes open. Both read the player perfectly through the classic pairs; on the gas they split, and the player's own verdict decides which theory loses. *Deliberating:* the player's own judgement. *Discovery:* "I'm not a double-effect person; what I care about is harm done to help." *Fun:* anticipation, discovery. *Replay:* little.

The walkthrough for B lands the discovery quickly and cleanly, then has nothing for the player to want. A has the player reason inside each rule, twice per scheme, and lands the same discovery as a victory.

## 10. The pick, and why

**A, Foot's Bad Man**, the reckless one. It gives the stranger the best five minutes: a villain to play, an obvious first move that works (and is the paper's own argument against counting), a second opponent who resists it, and a hole the player finds by reading the rule and thinking — which is exactly the hole Foot found. The third opponent is the paper's own answer, and it holds; that failure is the result, named at the moment it happens. The famous tram turns out to be the one scheme that never works, which a stranger will remember.

**Author test.** Foot would recognise the move: the bad man and the philosopher both build cases that make a rule give a terrible answer; the game hands the player her method with the villain's motive. An economist would recognise it too: a rule is a mechanism, and the test of a mechanism is what an adversary can make it do. Both would enjoy being the bad man.

**Runner-up: B.** Switch if the villain frame read as glib in the build, or if the stalls' verdicts felt like an arbitrary oracle rather than the paper's "most of us"; B shows the same split with no villain and no stakes.

**What was risked.** The player is the villain, whose victories are other people's deaths; the paper's levity ("the levity of the examples is not meant to offend", p. 5) licenses a melodrama, not gore, so deaths are silhouettes falling and fading. The third reel is designed to be unwinnable. The audience is an oracle: its verdicts are the paper's sentences, cited in the mechanism file and on About.

## 12. Controls, mechanism, feedback, moment

- **Controls:** one, with a preview: tap a scheme card (the stage draws its situation and states the target's two options, without saying which they'll take); SPRING IT commits. Tried schemes for the current target are spent (dimmed, with their outcome mark).
- **Mechanism:** each scheme is a case with three features the paper uses — is the one's death *aimed at* (an end or means of the act); is the act *harm* to the one or *withheld help*; is it traded against *harm the agent would otherwise do*, or against *help* — plus *doomed anyway* (the cave). Each rule is a function of the features → act. The audience's verdict is a function of (scheme, act) → cheer / blameless / disgrace, each value tagged to the paper's sentence.
- **Feedback:** the film (the choice made, the deaths), the game's own intertitle (what happened, why by the rule, what the audience did), the good person's line, the stalls (stand and cheer; hiss the villain; turn on the hero, red), the portrait card.
- **Moment:** Doctor Double brews; the fumes go under the door; the stalls turn; red. The player's hypothesis ("they hate it when someone means to kill") has just been beaten by their own scheme.

- **Anscombe's reply** (the medicine: giving the whole dose to the one wrongs no one): on About.

- **The merchants and the gravediggers** (p. 2): both murderers; makes the point that DDE does not excuse every foreseen harm. On About.

## 15. Divergences

- **A bad man behind every case.** In the paper only the torture/murderer case has one. In the game he poisons the five, cuts the brakes, floods the cave. This keeps each case's structure (who threatens whom, what the agent's act does) — the five's danger is still not the agent's doing except in the tram, where the agent drives — and Foot's own rule treats preventing others' harm as aid ("to refrain from inflicting injury ourselves is a stricter duty than to prevent other people from inflicting injury", p. 4). Ours, declared.
- **One against five everywhere.** The paper uses one-for-five for the tram, the judge (five hostages), the drug and the torture; "two" for the mad murderer; unnumbered for the serum, gas and beggar; the cave's party is unnumbered. Ours, to hold the numbers fixed as Foot does in her pairs.
- **The bargain stands in for the judge, the serum and the torture**: one aimed killing to bring aid. The judge's corruption of justice is removed as Foot removes it.
- **The cave from outside.** In the paper the trapped party, the agents, blast their way out; here the target holds the dynamite outside and five are trapped behind the stuck man. Ours: it keeps one against five and the target's own life out of it. "Fat man" becomes "a man stuck fast".
- **The audience's verdicts** are the paper's ("we", "most of us", "most reasonable men", the relatives who would sue). Where the paper gives a verdict only on the act, the verdict on refusing is ours, derived from the paper's own line that the bad man, not the refuser, intends those deaths (p. 3).
- **Three named good people.** Mr Most and Doctor Double are ours; they stand for the views the paper discusses. Mrs Foot is Philippa Foot's own principle, under the name Anscombe uses for her.
- **The rules as mottos.** DDE and Foot's principle are compressed into two or three lines each; the cave reveals a qualification of Foot's ("no serious conflict of interests" when the one is lost either way) that her motto does not state. Ours, declared on About.
- **Deaths** are silhouettes that fall and fade. The paper's examples are lethal; the game does not show more than the paper says.

## 16. Mechanism kind and claim boundaries

**Toy.** The paper hands over an argument: paired cases, two rival principles, and the verdicts "we" give. `mechanism.js` describes each scheme by the four features the argument uses (is the one's death aimed at or only foreseen; is the act harm done to him or help withheld; is it traded against harm the agent would do or against help; would he die anyway) and runs each rule as a function of them. The audience's verdict on each act is a lookup, each entry tagged with the paper's sentence. There is no number to fit.

- **Demonstrates:** that the three rules, as stated, answer the paper's six cases as the paper says they do, and that the double-effect rule and Foot's part on the gas (the brew) and the cave and nowhere else among them.
- **Assumes:** the bad man behind every case; one against five throughout; the rules compressed into mottos; the audience's verdicts on refusals (ours, from p. 3: the bad man, not the one who refuses him, intends those deaths); that "most of us" is a single audience.
