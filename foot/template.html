Bad Man

A melodrama in seven scenes, from the 1967 paper by Philippa Foot in which the runaway tram first appears

You know the runaway tram. You probably think the lesson is about what you mean to do. Foot, who invented the tram, thought otherwise. In this film Mr Bad asks you to do seven things, each a worse deed than the last, to save a few people from him. You decide each one. Then the house tells you how she reads it, and where she draws her line. It is not where you think.

Elizabeth Anscombe's note "Who is Wronged?" ran straight after the paper, disputing one point: that you must give the medicine to the five. Give the whole bottle to the one who needs it, and who can say you wronged him? Nobody was owed it. If you gave it to the one man, she is on your side.

How to play

Seven things Mr Bad asks of you. Decide each one, and find Foot's line.

Who you are. Yourself. Mr Bad has put you where you can save some people from him by doing something to one other person. Seven times. The numbers hardly change; what he asks you to do gets worse each time: turn a wheel, keep a bottle, brew a cure, shut a door, pull a trigger, take up the irons, light a fuse.

What you do. Read what he says. Look at the picture. Tap Do it or Refuse. The film shows what happened.

Then the house speaks. The people along the bottom of the picture watch what you do, and react to it. They don't vote. After each scene they tell you how Philippa Foot reads it: what you would be doing to the one person, what the others would get from you, what kind of conflict that makes, which of her rules decides it, and whether she would have done what you did. Twice, at the brew and at the cave, they also tell you what the rule you probably came in with, the one about what you mean to do, would have done. Mr Bad gets one line.

The step before. Each scene is paired with an earlier one that has the same shape. If you decided the two differently, the house asks you what the difference was, and tells you whether that is the difference Foot names.

The staircase along the top shows where you are and what you did: amber for did it, a hollow mark for refused.

The intermission. The whole staircase with four lines on it: yours, Foot's, the intention rule's, and Bentham's. Then an epilogue: the three cases about abortion the paper was written for. Then the curtain.
