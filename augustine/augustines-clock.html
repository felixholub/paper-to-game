How to play

Measure how long things last, with no clock but your mind.

The circle is now. In the middle of the screen is a black circle on a black line. It is the present moment. Above the line is what is still to come, drawn dashed. Below the line is memory: what is over, drawn solid.

Nothing is drawn while it lasts. While a voice sounds, the circle turns red, and that is all. When the voice stops, a red bar drops into memory. While you hold, the circle turns blue. When you let go, a blue bar drops in under the red one, so you can see which was longer.

A round. Touch the circle to hear the voice. When it stops, press and hold the circle as long as the voice lasted, and let go. The game says how close you came: the same, nine tenths, half, double.

Outside. Some rounds have a potter's wheel turning, or the sun climbing. You can measure by them if you like. Watch what they do.

The silence. Once, you don't hold: you keep silent as long as the voice lasted, and tap the circle once when the silence is over.

The hymn. A voice sings eight syllables, short, long, short, long. You sing them back by holding the circle once for each, the long ones twice as long as the short ones. Then you touch one short and one long of yours to lay one against the other.

The last round asks for the very first voice again. Then the last page says what you did and what Augustine says about it.

About

The game in one line

Seven times a voice sounds and stops, and you measure it with nothing but your mind: you hold a circle as long as the voice lasted, keep a silence as long, or sing a hymn's line back. Outside, a potter's wheel turns or the sun climbs, the clocks a learned man told Augustine time was made of; inside, what is over drops into memory, and nothing is drawn while it lasts.

What the book says

In Book XI of the Confessions Augustine asks what time is. "If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not." The past is no longer, the future is not yet, and the present has no length: a hundred years cannot all be present, nor a year, nor a day, nor an hour, and any instant with any length at all would already be part past and part to come. Yet we measure times and compare them, and say this one was double that.

A learned man told him that the motions of the sun, moon and stars are time. Augustine did not agree. If the heavens stopped and a potter's wheel ran round, we would still say its turns were even, or that some were longer and some shorter, and we would say it by time. When the sun stood still for a battle, the battle still took its time. So time is not the motion of a body: we measure a body's motion, and its rest, by time.

Nor can a sounding voice be measured: not before it sounds, since it is not yet; not while it sounds, since it has not ended; not after, since it is gone. In the line "Deus Creator omnium" the second, fourth, sixth and eighth syllables are each double the others, and he hears it so; but the long one only begins when the short one has stopped. What he measures, then, is not the syllables but "something in my memory, which there remains fixed". "It is in thee, my mind, that I measure times." A silence is measured the same way, by stretching thought to the measure of a voice. Saying a psalm by heart, the mind expects the whole, attends to each syllable as it passes, and remembers what is said, until the expectation is used up and all of it is memory; and the same holds for every syllable of it, for the longer action it is part of, for a whole life, and for all the ages of men. Time, he concludes, seems to be a stretching (distentio, Pusey's "protraction") of the mind itself.

Book X, read here only for its account of memory: the senses bring in images of things, and the images stay in "the fields and spacious palaces of my memory" when the things are gone; what is not called to mind for a while glides back into "deeper recesses" and must be drawn together again to be known.

Mechanism: a toy, built to illustrate the book's argument

This game demonstrates, under its own assumptions, how the argument works when you are the one measuring. A voice can be matched only after it has stopped, against what it left in you. A wheel whose pace changes misleads anyone who counts its turns, and anyone who sees it change sees that with something that isn't the wheel. A sun that stands still stops nothing: you go on measuring. And a long syllable can be laid against a short one that stopped before it began, in memory. Counting a moving body fails here by exactly as much as its pace changed, whatever pace we give it; a hold made from the voice in memory does not.

It assumes (all ours): every length (voices of about two and a half to four seconds, a short syllable of under half a second); the wheel's three turns per voice, and the kick that doubles or halves its pace the moment you press; how far the sun climbs; the hymn's melody, which is not the Ambrosian chant; the words for how close you came, and the two readings on the last page (whether your hold looks like counting the wheel, or waiting for the sun). The game's referee is the phone's own clock, which Augustine would call one more motion measured by time. Every number sits in the config block at the top of the source, with its source or the reason for it.

The book establishes an argument, not a measurement: that the present has no length, that the past and future are only in the mind as memory and expectation, that time is not the motion of any body, and that we measure times in the mind. How close your holds came is no evidence for any of this, or against it. Augustine starts from the fact that we do measure; the game lets you do it.

What is cut

The opening prayers and the account of creation by the Word (XI.i–ix). The question what God was doing before he made heaven and earth (XI.x–xiii, xxx), and God's knowledge, which has no expectation and no memory (XI.xxxi): one line each on the last page. Foretelling the sunrise from the daybreak (XI.xviii). The lament that life is a scattering, and the hope to be gathered (XI.xxix). From Book X, everything but memory's store: the temptations of the senses, the happy life, forgetfulness, the lost coin. And all of Augustine's life.
